6: Abstract
7: Flexible and mechanically compliant energy-storage systems are essential for the development of next-generation soft mechatronic technologies, yet maintaining reliable operation under deformation, dehydration, and interfacial stress remains challenging. Hydrogels provide hydrated ionic pathways, tunable mechanics, adhesion, and opportunities for self-healing, while two-dimensional (2D) materials offer diverse electronic, electrochemical, and interfacial properties for engineering multifunctional composites. This review critically examines 2D material–hydrogel systems for flexible supercapacitors, microsupercapacitors, batteries, and hybrid energy storage devices. It considers graphene derivatives, MXenes, transition-metal dichalcogenides, layered double hydroxides, and carbon nitride, alongside emerging MBenes, 2D metal–organic frameworks, covalent organic frameworks, and related organic nanosheets. Beginning with hydrogel chemistry and the synthesis, surface functionality, and structural characteristics of 2D materials, the review connects constituent properties to network formation, ion transport, electronic conduction, and charge-storage mechanisms. Particular attention is given to nanosheet dispersion and restacking, polymer–filler interactions, dynamic crosslinking, pore accessibility, and solvent retention across hydrogel, organohydrogel, and related ionogel architectures. Comparative assessment distinguishes demonstrated hydrogel-based devices from adjacent electrode studies and emerging integration concepts, accounting for differences in conductivity, aqueous stability, processability, and evidence maturity. Applications in soft robotics, wearable electronics, smart textiles, and biomedical interfaces are evaluated through device-level requirements for mechanical compliance, electrochemical stability, encapsulation, and operational lifetime. The review further examines performance normalization, coupled mechanical–electrochemical–environmental testing, material-specific degradation, manufacturing safety, and biological and end-of-life considerations. By linking material chemistry and composite architecture to operating conditions and system integration, it identifies priorities for reproducible benchmarking, mechanistic aging studies, scalable fabrication, and application-appropriate degradation. This materials-to-systems perspective establishes design principles for reliable, sustainable, and application-matched energy storage in soft mechatronics.
8: Keywords: Two-dimensional materials; hydrogel nanocomposites; flexible energy storage; graphene derivatives; MXenes; two-dimensional organic frameworks; interfacial engineering; soft robotics; wearable electronics; bioelectronics.

10: 1. Introduction
11: Soft mechatronic systems require energy-storage units to function as integral components of deformable structures rather than rigid accessories. Their power sources must accommodate platform mechanics and circuitry while maintaining function. This extends evaluation beyond conventional metrics such as capacitance, energy density, and cycling stability. A material with high static capacitance may be unsuitable if electrical contact deteriorates during deformation, dehydration disrupts ion transport, or rigidity restricts actuation. Conversely, hydrogels with moderate electrochemical performance may offer advantages through hydrated ionic pathways, adhesion, flexibility, and compatibility with skin, textiles, and compliant joints [1-3].
12: Combining hydrogels with two-dimensional (2D) materials offers a route to address these demands. This family includes MXenes, MBenes, graphene derivatives, transition-metal dichalcogenides, inorganic nanosheets, and organic nanosheets such as 2D metal–organic frameworks and covalent organic frameworks. Their structures, surface chemistries, porosities, electronic properties, and redox-active sites create opportunities for charge storage and transport. MXenes are important because their conductivity and surface terminations can support electrochemical activity in hydrated polymer networks; however, oxidation sensitivity and processing requirements make comparison with alternative 2D materials essential [4-6].

17: This review adopts a materials-to-systems framework linking hydrogel chemistry, 2D material structure and functionalization, interfacial interactions, composite-network formation, electrode architecture, and device integration. It considers how dispersion, nanosheet restacking, polymer–filler coupling, ionic transport, and dynamic crosslinking influence mechanical compliance and electrochemical performance. Flexible supercapacitors, microsupercapacitors, batteries, and hybrid energy-storage systems are examined alongside encapsulation and environmental-stability requirements.
18: Operational reliability is emphasized over isolated peak performance. Wearable devices must withstand sweat exposure, repeated bending, adhesion–reapplication cycles, and dehydration, while soft robots experience bending, torsion, compression, and repetitive actuation. Biomedical and biodegradable platforms additionally require assessment of cytocompatibility, sterilization tolerance, degradation kinetics, and decomposition products [13-15]. Capacitance, resistance, adhesion, hydration, and device geometry evolve together; electrode fracture, electrolyte displacement, or stiff encapsulation can undermine promising materials [2, 5, 16]. Accordingly, this review addresses material foundations, composite design, device performance, soft robotic and wearable applications, biomedical integration, and sustainability challenges. 19: 1.1 Flexible Energy Storage Needs in Soft Mechatronics
20: Soft mechatronics integrates sensing, actuation, computation, communication, and energy delivery within mechanically deformable structures. Robotic grippers undergo bending, stretching, compression, and torsion, while skin-mounted electronics experience repetitive body movements, external pressure, perspiration, and temperature fluctuations. Energy storage components must therefore maintain electrode–electrolyte contact, ionic conductivity, electrical safety, and operational stability during complex deformation [2, 16, 17].
21: The increasing demand for distributed autonomy further highlights the importance of localized energy storage. Soft robots and wearable devices require power for sensing, data acquisition, wireless communication, memory, and intermittent actuation. Flexible microsupercapacitors provide rapid power delivery, hydrogel-based batteries support extended operation, and hybrid systems enable localized energy generation, storage, and utilization [18-20]. However, miniaturization alone cannot eliminate mechanical and interfacial incompatibilities between conventional energy storage components and compliant substrates. Rigid batteries introduce stress concentrations, liquid electrolytes present leakage risks, and dry solid electrolytes may exhibit excessive stiffness or limited ionic conductivity. Hydrogel nanomaterials offer an alternative by retaining electrolytes within flexible, hydrated, and conformable polymer networks [1, 21]. Consequently, flexible energy storage is a fundamental requirement governing autonomy, safety, reliability, and operational lifetime. Soft robots require distributed power for actuation and feedback control, wearable electronics demand lightweight and skin-conformal modules, and biomedical interfaces require mechanical compatibility, biocompatibility, stable ionic exchange, and potentially controlled degradability [2, 13].
22: These applications impose distinct electrochemical and mechanical requirements. Electronic skin benefits from rapid charging, low interfacial impedance, and prolonged bending endurance; robotic actuators require pulse-power capability and deformation-compatible encapsulation; and transient biomedical sensors require predictable degradation into nontoxic products. Such demands have stimulated interest in hydrogels, MXenes, graphene derivatives, conductive polymers, and hybrid architectures [16, 22]. Performance assessment must therefore reflect the intended application. Areal energy density may be more informative than gravimetric capacitance for epidermal devices, whereas volumetric performance and pulse-power response are particularly relevant to embedded robotic systems. Likewise, biomedical suitability requires evaluating the complete device, including electrodes, current collectors, encapsulation, and degradation products, rather than electrolyte biocompatibility alone [13, 14, 17].
23: 1.2 Hydrogels and Two-Dimensional Nanomaterials as Enabling Materials
24: Hydrogels offer tunable physicochemical and mechanical properties across molecular, network, and mesoscopic scales. Monomer chemistry influences charge density, hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and degradation behavior. Crosslinking regulates structural stability, energy dissipation, and self-healing, whereas porosity, filler distribution, and solvent composition affect ionic transport and fatigue resistance. These characteristics enable hydrogels to function as electrolytes, adhesive interfaces, substrates, encapsulants, and sensing components [23-25].
25: MXenes complement hydrogel networks through their two-dimensional conductive structures and chemically active surfaces. Their general formula, Mₙ₊₁XₙTₓ, represents transition-metal carbides, nitrides, or carbonitrides with surface terminations such as –O, –OH, –F, and –Cl. These groups facilitate dispersion in polar media and interactions with polymer chains, water molecules, and ions. MXenes contribute to electrochemical energy storage through electric double-layer capacitance, pseudocapacitive reactions, ion intercalation, and efficient electronic transport [26-29].
26: The primary design challenge involves regulating interactions between hydrated polymer networks and conductive MXene nanosheets. Insufficient interfacial interactions promote aggregation and restacking, while excessively strong interactions may restrict interlayer accessibility and ionic transport. Similarly, excessive water may intensify dehydration-related instability and limit electrochemical voltage stability, whereas insufficient hydration reduces ion mobility. Effective composites therefore require coordinated optimization of water content, MXene loading, surface terminations, crosslinking density, electrolyte concentration, and encapsulation [4, 30, 31]. This interfacial balance governs simultaneous ionic and electronic conduction. Polymer networks control swelling, hydration, and mechanical energy dissipation, while MXene nanosheets establish conductive pathways and electrochemically active interfaces. Reversible interactions can preserve structural integrity during deformation, whereas uncontrolled aggregation limits ion-accessible conductive regions. Accordingly, MXene concentration, surface chemistry, polymer composition, and solvent characteristics must be optimized as interconnected design parameters [4, 30, 32].
27: 1.3 Why MXenes? Opportunities and Limitations of Other 2D Materials
28: MXenes combine conducting sheets, ion-accessible galleries, and polar polymer interfaces. Recent V₄C₃Tₓ/PVA electrolytes [33] and MXene-cross-linked polypyrrole electrodes [34] demonstrate different functions: electronic percolation supports an electrode but can short an electrolyte separator. Alternatives include MXene/rGO hydrogel electrodes [35], MoS₂ gel membranes [36], boride anodes [37], and MBene-containing colloidal electrolytes [38]. Selection must therefore consider electrode chemistry, hydration, processing, and deformation together. A constituent's conductivity does not establish superiority in a packaged hydrogel device, and a hydrated colloid does not demonstrate a persistent polymer network.
29: 1.4 Scope and Organization of the Review
30: This review integrates MXene synthesis, hydrogel chemistry, flexible electrochemistry, soft robotics, and wearable and biomedical electronics within a unified framework. The central objective is to evaluate materials and devices according to their combined mechanical, electrochemical, and application-specific performance rather than isolated material properties [2, 16, 39].
31: The discussion encompasses three interconnected levels. At the constituent-material level, hydrogel composition, MXene surface chemistry, and additional two-dimensional fillers determine fundamental transport and mechanical properties. At the composite-architecture level, nanosheet orientation, porosity, dynamic bonding, solvent composition, and interfacial compatibility govern mixed ionic–electronic transport. At the device-system level, electrodes, separators, electrolytes, current collectors, encapsulation, interconnects, energy harvesters, sensors, and circuits must operate as an integrated compliant platform [5, 20, 40]. Different material strategies address specific limitations: organohydrogels improve dehydration and freezing resistance; MXene/GO aerogels mitigate restacking and enhance porosity; dynamic crosslinks improve fatigue resistance and self-healing; printable MXene inks facilitate miniaturized device integration; and biodegradable composites support transient and sustainable applications [15, 31, 39, 41, 42].
32: The review therefore provides an application-oriented assessment of MXene–hydrogel flexible energy storage, encompassing MAX-to-MXene conversion, hydrogel network engineering, ion transport, charge-storage mechanisms, flexible supercapacitors, batteries, hybrid energy systems, soft robotics, wearable sensing, human–machine interfaces, smart textiles, and biomedical electronics. Section 2 introduces material foundations; Section 3 examines composite design and fabrication; Section 4 addresses mechanical, electrochemical, and integration requirements; Section 5 discusses flexible supercapacitors, microsupercapacitors, batteries, and hybrid systems; Section 6 explores soft robotics and self-powered wearable technologies; Section 7 evaluates biocompatible, biodegradable, and biomedical applications; and Section 8 critically compares reported systems, identifies unresolved challenges, and outlines future research directions.
33: 2. Material Foundations of Hydrogels and Two-Dimensional Materials
34: Hydrogel–MXene energy storage systems rely on interconnected electronic, ionic, and mechanical networks. Electronic conduction occurs through MXene sheets, conductive polymers, graphene derivatives, carbon nanotubes, and current collectors, while ionic transport is facilitated by water, salts, ionic liquids, polymer-bound charges, and interconnected pores. Mechanical integrity arises from polymer chains, crosslinking interactions, filler–polymer interfaces, and hierarchical architectures. Sustained connectivity among these networks during deformation and environmental exposure is essential for device stability [4, 21, 30]. Processing-induced structural variations significantly influence material performance. Vacuum-filtered MXene films offer high conductivity but limited stretchability, whereas freeze-cast MXene/graphene aerogels enhance ionic accessibility at the expense of packing density. Polymer-rich hydrogels improve flexibility and adhesion but may increase electronic resistance, while redox-active gels enhance capacitance yet promote self-discharge. Consequently, material optimization requires balancing competing electrochemical and mechanical properties [41, 43, 44]. Processing parameters, including sonication duration, washing procedures, centrifugation speed, freeze-casting orientation, printing shear rate, and post-gelation solvent exchange, influence flake dimensions, interlayer spacing, pore connectivity, and filler distribution. Therefore, reproducible performance evaluation requires comprehensive reporting of processing conditions, dispersion characteristics, filler loading, solvent content, and mechanical conditioning before electrochemical testing [41, 43, 45].
35: 2.1 Hydrogel Chemistry, Synthesis, Ionic Transport, and Mechanics
36: Hydrogels for energy storage are commonly fabricated through in situ polymerization, physical gelation, freeze–thaw processing, or post-gelation solvent/ion exchange. In situ polymerization enables filler incorporation and structural control but requires removal of residual monomers and initiators for biomedical applications. Physical gelation provides reversible network formation, whereas freeze–thaw processing generates crystalline domains, particularly in poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), enhancing toughness without extensive chemical cross-linking [21, 23, 46].
37: Hydrogel ionic conductivity depends on electrolyte concentration, water distribution, polymer-chain mobility, transport tortuosity, ion pairing, and filler–polymer interactions. MXene incorporation can enhance ionic transport through hydrophilic interfaces and interlayer water, although excessive loading may increase tortuosity and restrict water mobility [3, 47]. Free water facilitates ion diffusion but promotes evaporation and potentially MXene oxidation, whereas bound water improves retention at the expense of ion mobility. Intermediate water states, hydrogen-bond networks, and nanoconfined water further influence ionic transport. Consequently, conductivity evaluation should consider hydration, swelling, temperature, humidity, and mechanical deformation, including repeated bending, compression, dehydration, rehydration, and substrate attachment [3, 47, 48]. Mechanical compliance must satisfy application-specific requirements, including conformability for epidermal sensors, fatigue resistance for soft robotics, abrasion tolerance for textiles, and tissue-compatible stiffness for implantable devices. Combining covalent cross-links with reversible physical interactions, ionic coordination, and hydrogen bonding enables structural integrity, energy dissipation, and self-healing [2, 24, 25].

49: Polyrotaxane (PR)-based cross-linkers provide another approach to improving hydrogel extensibility. Functionalization of PR with 2-acryloyloxyethyl isocyanate produces PR-C, whose isocyanate groups react with α-cyclodextrin (α-CD) hydroxyl groups to form carbamate linkages. However, N-isopropylacrylamide (NIPA) hydrogels containing PR-C exhibited only modest mechanical improvements over conventional N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide (BIS)-cross-linked networks. Solvent exchange from dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to water also caused slight opacity, suggesting PR-C contraction due to limited solubility and α-CD aggregation, which restricts sliding-ring mobility [50]. Although alkaline conditions reduce aggregation, their practical applicability is limited. Hydroxypropylated PR cross-linkers (HPR-C), synthesized as illustrated in Fig. 2(c), improved transparency without substantially enhancing extensibility. Fig. 2(d) further illustrates NIPA–AAcNa–HPR-C hydrogel preparation using ionic comonomers. Thermoresponsive poly(NIPA) networks exhibit a volume transition near 32 °C [50]. Randomly incorporated ionic monomers generally increase the transition temperature, while excessive ionic content suppresses volume changes through strong hydration. Grafted ionic chains, however, largely preserve the poly(NIPA) transition. Accordingly, ionic PR-derived cross-linkers (iPR-C), illustrated in Fig. 2(e), were developed to enhance water compatibility and mechanical performance. Carboxyl-functionalized α-CD units improve cross-linker solubility, maintaining extended iPR-C configurations within neutral poly(NIPA) networks, as depicted in Fig. 2(f) and (g), thereby facilitating sliding interactions and mechanical extensibility.
50: Wood-derived hydrogels demonstrate hierarchical mechanical reinforcement through anisotropic architectures. Fig. 2(h) and (i) illustrate their aligned structure and reversible twisting deformation [51]. Following delignification, preserved cellulose nanofiber (CNF) frameworks are infiltrated with acrylamide (AM), ammonium persulfate (APS), and N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA). Polymerization at 60 °C generates a polyacrylamide (PAM) network reinforced by CNF–PAM hydrogen bonding, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (j). Tensile strength and elastic modulus reach 36 MPa and 310 MPa longitudinally, compared with 0.54 MPa and 0.135 MPa transversely, respectively. Unmodified PAM exhibits corresponding values of only 0.072 MPa and 0.01 MPa. This reinforcement also provides optical anisotropy, transparency, and nanofluidic ionic transport, supporting multifunctional biomedical applications [51].
51: Self-adhesion is particularly important for epidermal and organ-mounted sensing devices [52]. Fig. 2(k) illustrates adhesion through covalent interactions involving hydroxyl, ether, amino, carboxyl, and catechol functionalities, including imine and amide bond formation. Noncovalent mechanisms include hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, cation–π interactions, and mechanical interlocking. Hydrogel morphology further determines mechanical and transport characteristics. Fig. 2(l1)–(l3) present scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of OP, OPP, and OPPC hydrogels. OP exhibits an average pore size of 140 ± 80 μm, decreasing to 0.4 ± 0.4 μm following PVA/borax incorporation in OPP through increased dual-network cross-linking [53]. Additional carbon nanotube (CNT) incorporation in OPPC slightly reduces pore dimensions, potentially through hydrogen bonding with O-CMCS or PVA chains. Pore architecture also depends on polymer concentration and freezing conditions. Higher polymer concentrations restrict ice-crystal growth, while lower freezing temperatures generally produce finer pores through accelerated crystallization. Unlike conventional copolymerization, which produces irregular pore distributions, directional freezing controls pore dimensions and orientation through regulated ice-crystal growth, as illustrated in Fig. 2(l4) [53]. These relationships establish a structural basis for optimizing hydrogel networks toward integrated ionic conductivity, mechanical compliance, and functional stability.
52: 2.2 MXenes: Synthesis, Surface Functionalization, and Key Properties
53: MAX phases are layered, nanolaminated materials with the general formula Mₙ₊₁AXₙ, where M represents an early transition metal, A denotes an A-group element, and X typically represents carbon and/or nitrogen. The parameter n generally ranges from 1 to 3, corresponding to the 211, 312, and 413 structural families, while higher-order 514 phases have also been reported. As illustrated in Fig. 3(a), high-throughput computational screening provides an efficient strategy for identifying novel MAX phases and potential precursors for two-dimensional (2D) MXenes. Using density functional theory (DFT), the study investigated 1,122 candidate compositions involving 11 transition metals, 17 A-site elements, two X elements (C and N), and three structural types. Thermodynamic formability was evaluated by comparing candidate formation energies with those of competing phases, while phonon calculations assessed local dynamical stability. Of the candidates investigated, 466 were predicted to have a high probability of experimental realization, and 136 were identified as potentially exfoliable, corresponding to 26 single-transition-metal MXene compositions. Exfoliation feasibility was further assessed by considering exfoliation energy and the relative strengths of M–A and M–X bonds, helping identify candidates that may undergo selective A-layer removal while retaining their 2D frameworks [54].

70: The conversion of MAX phases into MXenes generally involves selective A-layer etching, followed by intercalation and delamination. Conventional hydrofluoric acid (HF) etching has motivated the development of alternative approaches, including LiF/HCl mixtures, ammonium bifluoride, molten-salt etching, Lewis-acidic etching, supercritical-fluid methods, and chemical vapor deposition. These techniques differ in etching efficiency, defect formation, surface termination chemistry, scalability, and dispersion stability [56, 57]. The resulting MXene surfaces commonly contain oxygen-, hydroxyl-, and halogen-containing terminations, such as –O, –OH, –F, and, depending on the processing route, –Cl. These surface groups influence hydrophilicity, interlayer spacing, electrical conductivity, oxidation stability, and interactions with polymer matrices [28, 30, 54, 55]. MXene flake dimensions, precursor quality, and etching conditions also affect hydrogel integration: larger flakes can enhance conductivity and mechanical reinforcement but may sediment or align during gelation, whereas smaller flakes can improve dispersion but may be more susceptible to oxidation. Surface modification, interlayer engineering, and hybridization with graphene, conductive polymers, metal oxides, layered double hydroxides, and carbon nanotubes can further improve structural stability and electrochemical performance [30, 41, 42, 56]. These relationships provide a foundation for designing conductive, mechanically robust, and electrochemically active 2D material–hydrogel composites for energy storage and related applications.

74: 2.3 MBenes: An Emerging Class of Two-Dimensional Transition-Metal Borides
75: MBenes are an emerging family of two-dimensional (2D) transition-metal borides attracting attention for their compositionally tunable electronic structures and potential applications in electrochemical energy storage. Their development parallels the production of MXenes from layered MAX phases, Mₙ₊₁AXₙ (n = 1–3), although MBenes originate principally from metal–aluminum–boron (MAB) precursors rather than carbide or nitride MAX phases. Research on MAX phases and MXene nanocomposites provides a useful structural comparison, particularly regarding precursor composition, selective etching, surface termination, and conductive electrode design [26, 27, 59, 60]. However, this analogy does not establish equivalent synthetic accessibility, chemical stability, or hydrogel compatibility. Unlike Ti₃C₂Tₓ MXene, whose surface groups are conventionally represented by Tₓ, MBene compositions and terminal groups must be established experimentally for each treatment. Consequently, a layered appearance or increased electrode capacitance cannot independently establish the formation of a chemically distinct, fully delaminated MBene phase.
76: MBenes are an emerging family of two-dimensional (2D) transition-metal borides attracting attention for their compositionally tunable electronic structures and potential applications in electrochemical energy storage. Their development parallels the production of MXenes from layered MAX phases, Mₙ₊₁AXₙ (n = 1–3), although MBenes originate principally from metal–aluminum–boron (MAB) precursors rather than carbide or nitride MAX phases. Research on MAX phases and MXene nanocomposites provides a useful structural comparison, particularly regarding precursor composition, selective etching, surface termination, and conductive electrode design [26, 27, 59, 60]. However, this analogy does not establish equivalent synthetic accessibility, chemical stability, or hydrogel compatibility. Unlike Ti₃C₂Tₓ MXene, whose surface groups are conventionally represented by Tₓ, MBene compositions and terminal groups must be established experimentally for each treatment. Consequently, a layered appearance or increased electrode capacitance cannot independently establish the formation of a chemically distinct, fully delaminated MBene phase. MAB phases exhibit diverse compositions, including M₂AB₂, M₂A₂B₂, M₃A₂B₂, M₄AB₄, M₃AB₄, and M₄AB₆, with orthorhombic or hexagonal structures containing distinct boron-chain arrangements. In-plane ordered i-MAB derivatives, exemplified by Mo₄/₃Y₂/₃AlB₂ and Mo₄/₃Sc₂/₃AlB₂, further expand their structural diversity. The stronger transition-metal–boron framework relative to selectively removable Al-containing layers provides a basis for preparing boride-rich sheets. As illustrated in Fig. 4(a,b), variations in boron connectivity can generate different 2D structural motifs [29]. Nevertheless, partially depleted MAB intermediates should not automatically be described as exfoliated MBenes. Reliable identification requires elemental analysis, diffraction, nanosheet thickness measurements, and surface-chemistry characterization to establish the extent of deintercalation and structural preservation. Preferential Al extraction may introduce defects, change electronic transport, and expose reactive surface sites. Residual Al content and oxidation should therefore be evaluated before relating electrode performance to intrinsic properties of the proposed MBene framework, particularly when comparing different etchants or treatment durations.
77: MBene synthesis can involve top–down wet-chemical etching, molten-salt processing, and high-temperature dealloying, whereas physical or chemical vapor deposition represents a possible bottom–up approach (Fig. 4(c)) [29]. In one reported MoB-related route, MoO₃ (2 g), Al (1 g), and B (3.5 g) were ground for 3 h, pelletized, and annealed under Ar at 1150 °C for 6 h using a heating rate of 180 °C h⁻¹. Subsequent ultrasonication in 15, 20, or 25 wt% NaOH for 6 h preceded hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 48 h. The products were repeatedly washed, centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min per cycle, and dried at 80 °C overnight, yielding samples designated MoB-15, MoB-20, and MoB-25. Figure 4(d,e) presents this processing sequence alongside the preparation of a MoB-derived hydrogel architecture (MoBH) and its lyophilization [56]. These procedures illustrate how precursor processing and removal of Al-containing species influence the resulting material, but do not alone verify complete exfoliation.
78: Electrochemical studies further demonstrate the importance of composition and device architecture. Fluoride-free MoAl₁₋ₓB supercapacitor electrodes [58], layered MoAlB/Mo₂AlB₂ lithium-storage electrodes [61], microwave-prepared MoB sodium-ion anodes [62], and boride-based sulfur hosts [63] illustrate distinct charge-storage functions. In the system shown in Fig. 4(f–h), treating MoAlB with 1 wt% NaOH for 24 h at room temperature achieved approximately 25% Al deintercalation, producing hydroxyl-containing MoAl₁₋ₓB surfaces. The resulting 1/24-MoAl₁₋ₓB film electrode exhibited an areal capacitance of 2006.60 mF cm⁻², with both surface-controlled and diffusion-controlled charge-storage contributions [58]. Its all-solid-state supercapacitor incorporated a separate poly(vinyl alcohol)–LiCl (PVA–LiCl) gel electrolyte. Similarly, the boride electrodes reported by Aghamohammadi et al. employed a PVA/H₂SO₄ gel electrolyte [64]. Neither configuration, by itself, demonstrates a uniformly MBene-filled polymer hydrogel.
79: Recent MoOₓ/MoB anodes [37] and hydrated Mo₄/₃B₂T₂ colloidal zinc electrolytes [38] further distinguish borides used as electrochemically active electrodes from those employed to regulate ionic transport. For comparison, Fig. 4(i,j) illustrates cation-mediated gelation of negatively charged metallic-phase MoS₂ sheets, producing an ion-stabilized membrane, F-MoS₂-H, containing approximately 85 wt% liquid. Its interconnected transport pathways support electrochemical operation at mass loadings reaching 11.2 mg cm⁻² [36]. Although this MoS₂ system demonstrates a hydrated 2D network, its mechanism cannot be assumed to apply directly to MBenes. Future MBene–hydrogel studies should therefore establish nanosheet retention, oxidation resistance, electronic percolation, swelling behavior, and long-term mechanical stability. Performance comparisons must distinguish active boride electrodes, separate gel electrolytes, hydrated colloids, and genuinely integrated boride–polymer networks rather than attributing device-level improvements indiscriminately to hydrogel incorporation.
80: 2.4 Other Two-Dimensional Materials for Hydrogel-Based Energy Storage
81: Beyond MBenes, graphene-based frameworks [35], LDH/carbon-hydrogel electrode pairs [65], MoS₂ hydrogel membranes [36], MoS₂-assisted polymer networks [66], and carbon-nitride/MXene heterostructures [67] offer complementary design approaches. Their functions and electrochemical metrics depend on whether the 2D material constitutes an electrode, a conductive scaffold, or a component of the hydrated polymer network. Accordingly, evidence from energy storage must be distinguished from related sensing and interface studies. This distinction also prevents misleading comparisons when cycling stability, specific capacity, or flexibility arise from different measurement protocols and cell configurations.
82: 2.4.1 Graphene and Graphene Oxide
83: Graphene-derived materials are attractive for hydrogel-based devices because they can establish conductive networks and reinforce polymer frameworks. Oxygen-containing groups promote graphene oxide (GO) dispersion and polymer interactions, whereas reduction increases the fraction of conductive carbon domains while altering available binding sites. Consequently, sheet dimensions, defect density, reduction degree, and electrolyte accessibility jointly influence performance. Network continuity can enhance electron collection, but excessive sheet stacking may restrict accessible surface area and impede ion diffusion, offsetting gains in bulk conductivity. Graphene-quantum-dot/graphene assemblies [68] and nitrogen-doped MXene/reduced graphene oxide (rGO) hydrogels [35] illustrate interfacial and scaffold engineering. ZnMoO₄/rGO photo-rechargeable electrodes [69] and NiCo-LDH/carbon-hydrogel electrode pairs [65] demonstrate alternative distributions of electroactive and conductive components. 
84: Importantly, sodium-polyacrylate gel electrolytes coupled with rGO electrodes [70] differ structurally from nanosheet-filled polymer hydrogels. MXene@polypyrrole/rGO hydrogels employ conducting-polymer connections [71], while GO-assisted systems have been investigated for electrolyte self-healing [72]. Likewise, rGO/polyacrylamide hydrogels demonstrate low-temperature and sensing functionality [73], but these results cannot independently establish superior storage performance. Meaningful comparisons must account for polymer content, network porosity, electrolyte retention, and device configuration rather than attributing capacitance changes solely to conductivity.
85: 2.4.2 Transition-Metal Dichalcogenides (TMDs)
86: Transition-metal dichalcogenides, particularly MoS₂, exhibit phase- and layer-dependent electrochemical behavior. Ionic gelation has enabled thick MoS₂ hydrogel membranes for capacitive storage [36], whereas GO-gel-coated MoS₂/MoO₂ composites have served as sodium-ion anodes [74]. Because their charge-storage mechanisms and reported metrics differ, direct capacitance–capacity rankings are inappropriate. Mechanically activated MoS₂-assisted polymerization [66] additionally demonstrates polymer-network modification rather than equivalent electrochemical performance.
87: Ion-coordinated PVA–MoS₂ photodetectors [75], printable alginate/MoS₂/silver-nanowire sensors [76], and multifunctional bioelectrical hydrogels [77] provide insights into processing and mixed transport. Nevertheless, their sensing performance should not be extrapolated to energy-storage devices. Future investigations should assess phase stability, scaffold contributions, and coupled mechanical–electrochemical durability under comparable conditions. Phase changes, layer restacking, and electrolyte-induced degradation also warrant examination because they may obscure the contribution of the original MoS₂ structure during prolonged cycling. Evidence for flexible TMD–hydrogel storage remains heterogeneous, and MoS₂ findings cannot be assumed to apply to WS₂.
88: 2.4.3 Other Emerging 2D Nanosheets 
89: Layered double hydroxides (LDHs) offer composition-tunable redox sites but generally benefit from conductive scaffolds. NiCo-LDH/rGO paired with carbon-hydrogel electrodes [65], GO/MnO₂/Co–Ni LDH hybrids [78], and cerium-containing LDH arrays [79] exemplify different architectures. Carbon nitride provides nitrogen-rich interfaces, as illustrated by sulfur-doped carbon-nitride/MXene composites [67], photo-rechargeable interfaces [80], fluorine-doped porous electrodes [81], and oxide/LDH hybrids [82]. Although these studies demonstrate promising heterostructure strategies, they do not necessarily validate polymer-hydrogel integration. Hydrogel-specific assessments of nanosheet retention, component release, mechanical fatigue, chemical compatibility, and long-term electrochemical stability remain necessary before these materials can be considered established load-bearing or multifunctional hydrogel fillers. Suitable controls should isolate each nanosheet's contribution from that of the polymer matrix and conductive additives, including mechanical testing before and after cycling.
90: 2.5 Two-Dimensional MOFs, COFs, and Related Organic Nanosheets for Hydrogel-Based Energy Storage
91: Two-dimensional organic frameworks extend hydrogel composite design beyond inorganic sheets by allowing coordination environments, covalent linkages, and molecular recognition sites to be selected independently. Their attraction is chemically addressable porosity rather than an assumed combination of high conductivity and aqueous stability. A layered crystal is not necessarily an isolated nanosheet: thickness distributions, lateral dimensions, and stacking must establish the dimensionality actually incorporated into a device. Likewise, a hydrated electrode scaffold, a polymer hydrogel electrolyte, and a dried framework aerogel represent different material states. This distinction is essential when transferring results from framework electrochemistry to flexible hydrogel systems [83, 84]. Fig. 5 highlights studies linking molecular architecture to integration opportunities while critically outlining the experimental limitations and scope of each approach.
92: 2.5.1 2D MOFs: Coordination Networks, Pore Architecture, and Redox/Electronic Transport
93: In layered MOFs, metal–ligand connectivity defines the in-plane network, while interlayer interactions control stacking and access to channels. Conjugated ligands coordinated to suitable metal centres can support electronic delocalization, whereas many carboxylate and imidazolate frameworks remain poorly conducting. Fig. 5(a) reproduces the layered Ni3(HITP)2 structure, illustrating coordination-linked sheets and accessible pore space [85]. Its source investigates oxygen reduction, so the structural illustration supplies no hydrogel-storage performance evidence. Additive-free Ni3(HITP)2 supercapacitors nevertheless establish that an intact conductive framework can serve directly as a charge-storage electrode [86]. Such results should be separated from pyrolysed MOF-derived carbons, whose coordination chemistry and electronic structure have changed.
94: Solvothermal growth, interfacial assembly, substrate-directed synthesis, and exfoliation offer different compromises between crystallinity, thickness, yield, and electrical contact. Direct growth can reduce binder resistance, but substrate coverage and adhesion may limit scale. Exfoliation exposes sites and shortens diffusion distances while introducing defects, fragmentation, and restacking during drying. The conductivity of a pressed powder also includes grain-boundary and contact contributions; it cannot simply be assigned to a dispersed sheet in a swollen gel. A systematic Ni3(HITP)2 comparison showed that morphology can govern capacitance more strongly than bulk conductivity [87]. Hierarchical ion pathways and interfacial contact therefore require optimization alongside intrinsic electronic transport.
95: Redox-active metals and ligands may provide Faradaic storage, but a metal-containing framework does not automatically behave pseudocapacitively. In Ni3(HITP)2 with aqueous NaTFSI, operando scattering supports persistent anion anchoring and predominantly sodium-ion redistribution, rather than ion intercalation [88]. This electrolyte-specific mechanism underlies Fig. 5(g). In alkaline systems, coordination networks can also reconstruct into hydroxides or oxyhydroxides; post-cycling diffraction alone may miss a thin active surface layer. Operando spectroscopy, metal-release measurements, and appropriate precursor controls are needed before assigning capacity to the original framework. These distinctions become especially consequential when hydrogel ligands compete for metal sites or change the local pH.
96: 2.5.2 2D COFs: Linkage Chemistry, Conjugation, and Electroactive Pores
97: COFs replace coordination bonds with extended covalent connectivity, enabling pore geometry and electroactive functionality to be programmed through monomer selection. Boronate linkages offer structural order but can be hydrolytically vulnerable; imine stability depends on substitution and electrolyte conditions. β-Ketoenamine, imide, and carbon-linked architectures offer alternative stability profiles rather than universal aqueous resistance. Exfoliation, interfacial polymerization, and confined growth can produce thin layers, but thickness and crystallinity require independent verification [83]. A low-angle diffraction peak alone neither proves monolayer formation nor establishes continuous through-plane ion channels in the hydrated composite.
98: Electroactive carbonyls, diimides, quinones, and nitrogen-containing heterocycles introduce ion-coupled redox centres. Fig. 5(b) shows the redox-active diimide walls and channel architecture of DTP–ANDI–COF, whose integration with carbon nanotubes illustrates the need to connect insulating or weakly conducting framework domains electronically [89]. The source demonstrates lithium-battery electrodes, not hydrogel devices. Conjugation and ordered stacking can assist transport, but neither guarantees useful macroscopic conductivity. Polymer infiltration may improve wetting while obstructing precisely the pores intended to expose redox sites. Consequently, pore accessibility should be measured after composite formation rather than inferred from nitrogen adsorption on the isolated powder.
99: The COF/rGO aerogel route in Fig. 5(d) shows another useful division of labour: graphene provides a connected scaffold, while the anthraquinone-containing framework contributes redox functionality and modifies assembly [90]. Its hydrogel precursor is freeze-dried before electrode evaluation, making it processing evidence rather than proof of an operating polymer-hydrogel composite. Triazine frameworks broaden the chemistry through nitrogen-rich pores and robust linking motifs, although high-temperature synthesis can generate disorder that complicates structural assignments [91]. Across these systems, more redox groups increase theoretical capacity only if electrons and compensating ions can reach them at the relevant loading and charging rate.
100: 2.5.3 Other Organic 2D Nanosheets
101: 2.5.3.1 Conjugated Polymer Nanosheets and Organic Electronic Networks
102: Conjugated polymer sheets span covalently connected two-dimensional networks and thin assemblies of conventional polymer chains. These categories should remain distinct. Solid-state coupling of preorganized phenazine precursors produces a layered conjugated aromatic polymer that can be exfoliated, as shown by the synthesis scheme and microscopy in Fig. 5(c) [92]. By contrast, ice templating organizes PEDOT:PSS into sheets through confinement and phase segregation, without creating a crystallographically defined two-dimensional covalent lattice [93]. Both approaches offer accessible interfaces, but their transport anisotropy, defect populations, and processing histories differ.
103: Within a hydrogel, conducting polymers can bridge poorly connected framework particles or supply their own doping-related charge storage. Their conductivity depends on oxidation state, counterions, hydration, and pH, so dry-film values provide an incomplete benchmark. Repeated ion uptake can cause swelling, dedoping, and mechanical fatigue. Carbon or metal-oxide reinforcement may improve continuity but dilutes the organic fraction and introduces additional electrochemical contributions. Thus, framework-free polymer controls and equal-loading comparisons are essential. The flexible devices reported for ice-templated polymer composites demonstrate processability and device fabrication; they do not establish that every conjugated sheet remains conductive inside an independently crosslinked hydrogel [93].
104: 2.5.3.2 Hydrogen-Bonded Organic Frameworks and Molecular Assemblies
105: Hydrogen-bonded frameworks exploit directional noncovalent interactions, frequently combined with π-stacking, to organize electroactive molecules. Reversibility can facilitate assembly but also allows competitive water binding, dissolution, and reorganization. The BBQPH framework combines carbonyl and nitrogen-containing sites with intermolecular locking; its aqueous zinc-battery study supports coupled Zn2+/H+ storage [94]. Fig. 5(e) presents the hydrogen-bonded structure and discharge profiles with ion-storage assignments specific to the investigated electrolytes. The reported capacity of 498.6 mAh g−1 at 0.2 A g−1 is normalized to cathode active mass, not the packaged cell. Its liquid-electrolyte architecture establishes molecular-storage feasibility, while hydrogel incorporation remains a separate validation step. Stable cycling of one hydrogen-bonded assembly cannot establish wet stability for the entire material class.
106: 2.5.4 Hydrogel Integration: Interfacial Coupling, Ion Transport, and Flexible Device Roles
107: Hydrogel integration comprises several experimentally distinguishable architectures. Framework particles or sheets may reside within the electrolyte as ion-regulating fillers; a framework-rich electrode may contact a separate gel; or a hydrated, electronically connected framework composite may itself function as an electrode. These configurations distribute electronic conduction, ionic transport, and mechanical support differently. Framework-containing electrolyte films should remain electronically insulating across their thickness, whereas electrodes require continuous electron pathways. Adding a conductive sheet to an electrolyte therefore demands checks for leakage current and internal short circuits, alongside any increase in ionic conductivity.
108: Direct integration is exemplified by a sulfonic-acid-functionalized COF/PVA gel electrolyte developed for zinc-ion batteries [95]. The proposed function involves Zn2+ interactions and deposition regulation, rather than using the COF as a high-conductivity electrode. This is stronger evidence of a framework-containing hydrogel device than a powder electrode tested in liquid electrolyte. However, gel incorporation does not by itself establish exfoliated nanosheet morphology or resolve the individual contributions of sulfonation, polymer hydration, and framework porosity. Matched PVA, nonfunctionalized-COF/PVA, and sulfonated molecular-additive controls would make that attribution more convincing. Temperature-dependent durability should additionally distinguish electrolyte water retention from intrinsic framework heat resistance.
109: Hydrogen bonding between polymer hydroxyl or amide groups and framework surfaces can improve dispersion and stress transfer. Coordination of polymer carboxylates with exposed metal sites offers stronger anchoring but may compete with the original linker environment. Electrostatic association and π interactions can connect charged gels or conjugated scaffolds to organic sheets. Stronger coupling is not invariably beneficial: extensive adsorption may occlude redox sites, suppress swelling, or immobilize ions. Conversely, weak attachment can permit aggregation and filler migration during repeated deformation. Spectroscopic shifts should therefore be combined with swelling measurements, extraction experiments, and hydrated mechanical tests rather than treated as sufficient proof of a durable interface.
110: COF membranes that regulate interfacial water at zinc anodes provide adjacent mechanistic guidance [96]. They should be identified as protective interlayers unless the experiment also incorporates a hydrated polymer network. Similarly, a triazole-based covalent gel demonstrates that an organic gel can stabilize zinc plating, but it should not be relabelled a crystalline two-dimensional COF without structural evidence [97]. Such studies suggest how local coordination, water activity, and transport selectivity might be combined in hydrogel formulations. Their performance cannot be transferred quantitatively because polymer-bound water, free electrolyte, and confined framework water have different mobilities and reaction environments.
111: For ion transport, polar pore walls can alter solvation and ion partitioning, while macroporous hydrogel channels provide access between framework domains. These length scales must work together. A high salt concentration can increase charge-carrier density yet reduce mobility through ion association or network contraction. Increased filler loading can reinforce a gel while raising tortuosity or interrupting polymer continuity. Impedance-derived conductivity should be reported with thickness, electrode area, temperature, hydration history, and contact conditions. Transference numbers and diffusion measurements are especially useful when selective cation transport is claimed, because total conductivity alone does not identify which species carries current.
112: Fig. 5(f), adapted from an experimentally demonstrated alginate/PEDOT-containing hydrogel supercapacitor, provides a relevant device-level comparison [98]. Its electronically modified outer gel layers and ion-conducting separator demonstrate the spatial organization of distinct functional components within a hydrogel-based device. Importantly, the reported architecture does not incorporate MOF or COF materials; rather, it offers a conceptual basis for future designs in which framework nanosheets could be positioned near the electrode interfaces while retaining an electrically insulating gel core. In comparison, Fig. 5(d) illustrates a framework-containing hydrogel-to-aerogel processing strategy [90], whereas Fig. 5(g) presents an experimentally supported pore-charging mechanism in a liquid-electrolyte system [88]. Collectively, these examples provide complementary insights into material processing, device architecture, and charge-storage mechanisms without suggesting that an integrated framework–hydrogel energy-storage device has already been experimentally demonstrated.

116: 2.5.5 Critical Comparison: Conductivity, Wet Stability, Processing, and Device-Level Evidence
117: Conductive MOFs offer electronically addressable coordination networks, COFs offer modular covalent pore chemistry, and conjugated polymers offer accessible electronic pathways with comparatively flexible processing. Hydrogen-bonded assemblies add reversible molecular organization, but their persistence depends strongly on solvent and packing. None provides an unconditional advantage. Interfacial engineering of MOF/graphene electrodes reinforces the importance of contact chemistry and hybrid architecture [99], while COF supercapacitor literature shows how conductive supports and linkage selection affect utilization [100]. Comparisons should therefore identify the conducting phase, active redox phase, ion-bearing phase, and mechanical scaffold explicitly. A hybrid's performance should not be assigned entirely to its framework component.
118: Wet stability requires simultaneous retention of chemistry, accessible porosity, particle morphology, and interfacial contact. Dry-state crystallinity after cycling can coexist with leaching, local amorphization, or reconstruction. Relevant protocols include prolonged electrolyte soaking, leachate analysis, spectroscopy at controlled state of charge, and mechanical testing after electrochemical aging. The electrolyte used matters as much as nominal linkage strength: concentrated salts, acidic gels, and alkaline electrolytes impose different coordination and hydrolysis conditions. The COF electrochemistry literature provides a broad foundation for such chemistry-specific assessment, but demonstrations in fuel-cell membranes or nonaqueous batteries should remain separate from hydrogel-storage claims [84].
119: Electronic and ionic conductivities must also be separated experimentally. Blocking-electrode measurements can estimate ionic resistance, whereas electronic leakage requires an appropriate steady-state measurement and correction for polarization. Results obtained at different water contents should not be pooled into a single material ranking. In a mixed conductor, a smaller impedance semicircle may reflect improved electrode contact rather than faster transport inside framework pores. Hydrated microscopy, thickness-dependent impedance, and transport measurements before and after filler extraction can help discriminate these possibilities.
120: Processing and loading impose equally important limits. Solvothermal reactions, solvent exchange, catalyst removal, exfoliation, and freeze-drying can dominate cost and reproducibility. Scale-up should report isolated yield, batch variability, solvent consumption, and whether sheet dimensions survive mixing and gelation. At practical electrode thickness, increased loading can amplify ionic gradients and electronic resistance even when low-loading gravimetric performance appears excellent. Areal capacity, volumetric energy, active-material fraction, and complete-device mass are therefore needed alongside gravimetric metrics. The mass and volume of electrolyte, current collectors, separators, and encapsulation must be included when claiming device-level energy density.
121: A defensible evaluation sequence begins with isolated-sheet characterization, proceeds to the equilibrated composite, and finishes with a sealed two-electrode device. Each stage should retain a mass balance for active material, supporting conductor, and polymer. Dissolution controls should distinguish reversible ion binding from irreversible loss of organic molecules or metal nodes. Aging under open circuit is also necessary because electrochemical cycling alone can conceal calendar degradation and self-discharge. These measurements would clarify whether an apparently stable capacity originates from persistent framework chemistry or from compensating changes elsewhere in the composite.
122: Future progress depends on testing coupled failure modes rather than adding isolated functionality. Bending radius, deformation rate, cycle count, temperature, and water loss should accompany electrochemical retention data; a photograph of a bent device establishes configuration, not fatigue resistance. Particularly useful directions include oriented ion pathways, electronically connected framework electrodes with insulating gel cores, and chemically anchored interfaces that preserve pore accessibility. Biomedical applications additionally require formulation-specific extractables, cytotoxicity, and degradation-product evidence. Neither organic composition nor a natural-polymer matrix establishes biodegradability or biocompatibility. The immediate opportunity is reproducible architecture-specific validation that connects molecular design with sustained performance under realistic hydration, loading, and mechanical conditions.
123: 2.6 Comparative Structure–Property Relationships and Design Principles in 2D Material–Hydrogel Systems
124: The electrochemical performance of 2D material–hydrogel systems is governed by the interplay among conductive networks, ion-accessible pathways, surface chemistry, interlayer spacing, and mechanical integrity. Figure 6 illustrates representative fabrication strategies and structure–property relationships in MBene-, MXene-, and MoS₂-based systems, while Table 1 provides a broader comparison with other 2D material–hydrogel architectures. These systems differ in how they distribute the conductive skeleton, ion-bearing phase, electrochemically active sites, and mechanically supporting network, ultimately influencing charge transport, electrolyte accessibility, and structural stability.
125: MBene-based systems. Figure 6(a,b) illustrates the design of a flexible MoB MBene-based interdigitated supercapacitor electrode and the chemical etching route used to obtain 2D MoB from the MoAlB precursor. Selective removal of the Al-containing layers facilitates the formation of 2D MBene structures, while the interdigitated electrode geometry provides a compact configuration for electrochemical energy storage [64]. The electrode architecture and processing conditions influence the accessibility of active surfaces and the effectiveness of current collection. In comparison, MBene colloids can regulate ion transport through nanosheet interactions and colloidal organization without necessarily forming a polymer-based hydrogel network, demonstrating an alternative approach to controlling transport in 2D materials [38].
126: MXene–carbon hybrid hydrogels. Figure 6(c,d) presents the configuration of a zinc-ion hybrid supercapacitor and the synthesis of an N-doped Ti₃C₂Tₓ–reduced graphene oxide (RGO) hybrid hydrogel. Following selective etching of Ti₃AlC₂ and nitrogen doping, Ti₃C₂Tₓ is combined with graphene oxide (GO) and subjected to hydrothermal treatment in the presence of L-ascorbic acid. The resulting interconnected MXene–RGO architecture can improve electrical connectivity, reduce nanosheet restacking, and facilitate electrolyte-ion transport through the three-dimensional network [35]. These characteristics highlight the importance of combining conductive 2D sheets with a supporting carbon framework to balance electronic transport and ion accessibility.
127: Cation-mediated MoS₂ hydrogel membranes. Figure 6(e,f) illustrates the cation-mediated assembly and formation of the F-MoS₂-H membrane, whereas Fig. 6(g,h) presents its structural characterization. Cation intercalation partially screens electrostatic repulsion between negatively charged MoS₂ nanosheets and promotes interlayer association and ionic gelation. The reported interlayer spacing decreases from approximately 15.0 Å in the hydrated MoS₂-H membrane to 9.8 Å following BMIM-BF₄-mediated assembly. Changes in zeta potential further support the role of ionic interactions in regulating nanosheet assembly [36]. Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) reveal differences in stacking morphology and nanosheet organization between the dried D-MoS₂ and F-MoS₂-H membranes. These results demonstrate that controlled interlayer interactions and water retention can influence membrane structure, swelling resistance, and ion-transport pathways [36].
128: Broader structure–property relationships. Related LDH/carbon-hydrogel systems [65] and reduced graphene oxide/polyacrylate cells [70] further illustrate how the integration of conductive phases with supporting networks influences electrochemical and mechanical behavior. Across these architectures, surface functionalization and interfacial bonding regulate hydration and nanosheet interactions, while increased interlayer spacing can improve ion accessibility only when adequate electronic connectivity is maintained. Likewise, polymeric or carbon-based reinforcement can enhance structural integrity but may restrict swelling and ion mobility when the network becomes excessively dense. Therefore, optimizing 2D material–hydrogel systems requires balancing electronic conductivity, ionic transport, active-site accessibility, and mechanical stability rather than maximizing any single structural parameter. Figure 6 and Table 1 collectively summarize these design principles without implying a universal performance ranking across materials with different compositions, electrolytes, electrode configurations, and testing conditions.

135: 2.7 Structure–Property Relationships in Ion Transport and Charge Storage
136: The electrochemical performance of hydrogel–MXene composites depends on the simultaneous continuity of electron-conducting networks, ion-accessible hydrated domains, and mechanically resilient interfaces. Mobile ions migrate through interconnected aqueous pores, polymer-associated ionic regions, and interlayer galleries before reaching electrochemically active surfaces. Accordingly, high electronic conductivity alone cannot guarantee high capacitance: tightly restacked nanosheets may provide efficient electron transport while excluding electrolyte from substantial fractions of the electrode. Conversely, excessive swelling or porosity can increase ionic accessibility but decrease packing density, contact integrity, and volumetric energy storage. Rational design therefore requires coordinated optimization of gallery spacing, surface chemistry, pore connectivity, polymer cross-linking, and the electrode–electrolyte interface [5, 29, 41, 43, 101].
137:  Charge storage can arise from electric-double-layer charging, reversible surface or near-surface redox reactions, and ion intercalation; battery-type insertion or conversion reactions may also contribute in some hybrid systems. These mechanisms cannot be distinguished solely from a high capacitance value. Cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and scan-rate-dependent kinetic analyses provide complementary information on transport limitations and charge-storage contributions. Moreover, compression, stretching, and bending may redistribute pore water, shift nanosheet contacts, or change diffusion distances. Measurements conducted only in an undeformed state therefore cannot establish reliability for wearable devices or soft robotic systems [25, 32, 45, 101, 102]. Figure 7 connects these considerations to three experimentally reported strategies: conductive-polymer confinement, aluminum-assisted hydrogel self-assembly, and ionic-liquid-mediated MXene intercalation.
138: Cheng et al.[42]. developed an organic–inorganic hydrogel that integrates poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)(styrene sulfonate) (PEDOT), carboxyl-functionalized titanium carbide MXene (C-MXene), and poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) cross-linked with glutaraldehyde (GA) [42]. As outlined in Fig. 7(a), PVA was first dissolved in a PEDOT dispersion under heating and stirring. Addition of C-MXene promoted interactions with PEDOT and altered PEDOT chain conformation; GA subsequently cross-linked PVA to establish a mechanically compliant network. Acetic acid treatment reduced the insulating PSS fraction, enhancing continuity of the conductive component. An all-hydrogel supercapacitor was then constructed by placing a PVA–H₃PO₄ gel electrolyte between two conductive hydrogel electrodes. This architecture deliberately separates the ion-bearing electrolyte from the electronically conductive, mechanically deformable electrodes while allowing intimate contact across their interfaces.
139: The resulting morphology illustrates how nanoscale confinement influences macroscopic behavior. Photographs in Fig. 7(b) demonstrate deformation by bending and twisting, while cross-sectional SEM in Fig. 7(c) shows a layered, porous framework rather than the particulate morphology characteristic of the original PEDOT dispersion. According to the reported interpretation, PEDOT transitions toward more extended chains and occupies spaces between stacked C-MXene nanosheets. Electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, conductive-chain entanglement, and covalent cross-linking then support interconnected electron pathways and mechanical stress transfer [42]. This synergy is composition-sensitive: insufficient MXene limits conductive reinforcement, whereas excessive loading promotes aggregation and undermines homogeneous strain distribution. Thus, hydrogel elasticity and electrical conductivity must be evaluated together, instead of optimizing nanosheet content solely for its nominal conductivity.
140: A contrasting strategy uses aluminum-assisted reduction and self-assembly of MXene with graphene oxide (GO) to form a three-dimensional MXene@reduced graphene oxide (rGO) hydrogel [102]. In Fig. 7(d), the initial MXene/GO suspension remains fluid after Al powder is added, whereas introducing hydrochloric acid (HCl) triggers gelation. The acid participates in the Al-mediated process, but larger acid quantities accelerate gas generation and may damage network uniformity. The reported preparation therefore selected 80 μL HCl as a practical processing condition. Controls containing MXene without GO, or Al³⁺ ions instead of metallic Al, did not yield comparably self-supporting networks. These comparisons indicate that Al serves a role beyond simple ionic charge screening, while GO contributes to a continuous scaffold. Importantly, substituting Al³⁺ for Al powder caused flocculation rather than equivalent gel formation, highlighting the significance of the coupled reduction and assembly process [102].
141: Network integrity depended strongly on precursor proportions. In the reported compression observations, a specimen prepared with 0.15 mL GO collapsed under a 5 g load, whereas increasing GO to 0.30 mL improved shape retention. Raising the Al-powder content accelerated gelation but, at excessive concentrations, generated aggregation and residual particles. SEM observations in Fig. 7(e) show a comparatively compact MXene@rGO-Al-7.5 architecture before acid washing, followed by a more open network after removal of metallic Al and immersion in deionized water. GO-deficient assemblies displayed cracking, and excessive Al³⁺ promoted large agglomerates. These results underline an important structural trade-off: a sufficiently connected scaffold is needed to withstand handling, but excessively compact domains or processing residues can obstruct uniform electrolyte infiltration. The observations support improved accessibility as a plausible architectural benefit; they do not alone quantify the fraction of electrochemically utilized surface [102].
142: Diffraction and Raman analysis provide complementary evidence for assembly, reduction, and possible degradation. As shown in Fig. 7(f), the MXene-related (002) reflection in assembled gels shifts toward lower diffraction angles; reported peaks near 2θ = 6.28° indicate expanded layer periodicity consistent with intercalation, although spacing changes alone do not identify the inserted species unambiguously. Reflections near 39° and 45°, assigned to metallic Al, disappear after acid washing, supporting its removal within the sensitivity of XRD. Additional reflections assigned to TiO₂ in the Al³⁺-treated control raise concerns about concurrent MXene oxidation. Raman bands near 1375 and 1580 cm⁻¹ correspond to defect-associated D and graphitic G vibrations, respectively. The reported G-band shift from 1591.75 to 1576.42 cm⁻¹ and increase in I_D/I_G from 0.957 to 1.028 are consistent with structural changes accompanying GO reduction; neither metric alone proves complete chemical reduction. Taken together, SEM, XRD, and Raman measurements identify processing-dependent changes in porosity, layer arrangement, and carbon structure [102].
143: A third route targets the ion-accessible galleries themselves. Huang et al. investigated Ti₃C₂Tₓ prepared using 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate (BMImBF₄), which functions as both an etching medium and an intercalating species [103]. The Ti₃AlC₂ MAX precursor in Fig. 7(g) exhibits densely stacked layers, while the processed MXene in Fig. 7(h,i) displays a more open, accordion-like morphology. XRD patterns in Fig. 7(j) show displacement of the (002) reflection to 2θ = 6.63°, corresponding to an apparent interplanar spacing of approximately 13.3 Å for Cu Kα radiation. For comparison, the supplied study places conventional HF-etched material near 8.6°–8.9°. Incorporation of BMIm⁺ into expanded interlayer regions is consistent with the accompanying structural evidence. Nevertheless, an enlarged interlayer distance is not a direct measurement of ion-diffusion rate and must be interpreted alongside electrochemical measurements [103].
144: Surface analysis adds a necessary chemical dimension. The FTIR spectrum in Fig. 7(k) contains bands reported at 603, 700–750, 1033, 1238, 1609, 1716, 2896, and 3256 cm⁻¹, assigned in the source to Ti–C, Ti–O, C–F, –COOH, C–O, C=O, C–H, and –OH-related vibrations, respectively [103]. Because some infrared features overlap, these assignments should be regarded as supportive rather than individually conclusive without complementary surface analysis. Raman features in Fig. 7(l) also indicate changes associated with oxidized surface species. Together, interlayer expansion and surface functionalization influence wetting, accessible charge-storage sites, and electrode stability. The ionic-liquid-treated MXene is not itself described here as a polymer hydrogel; its relevance to hydrogel composites lies in demonstrating how controlled gallery chemistry may mitigate restacking and facilitate ion access.

209: 3. 2D Material–Hydrogel Composite Design and Fabrication
210: Hydrogel–MXene composite design depends on the functional role of MXene as an electrochemically active material, conductive filler, mechanical reinforcement, photothermal actuator, cross-linking mediator, or interfacial adhesive component. Electrode applications require high active-material loading and continuous electronic pathways, whereas sensors benefit from optimized conductive percolation. Biomedical applications additionally require careful consideration of cytotoxicity and oxidation stability [32, 106-108]. An effective design strategy prioritizes application-specific failure mechanisms. Hydrogel toughness and reversible bonding mitigate electrode cracking, whereas MXene loading, nanosheet alignment, and conductive-polymer incorporation improve electronic transport. Environmental degradation can be addressed through organohydrogel, ionogel, antioxidant, or encapsulation strategies, reducing the need for excessive filler incorporation [31, 32, 106].
211: Fabrication methods must accommodate device architecture and operating conditions. Casting and in situ polymerization enable bulk hydrogel fabrication, while freeze-casting generates oriented pores and anisotropic transport pathways. Printing and patterned deposition facilitate microsupercapacitors, interdigitated electrodes, wireless devices, and smart textiles. Solvent exchange enhances environmental tolerance, whereas layer-by-layer assembly enables spatial control over conductivity, hydration, and mechanical compliance [39, 45, 109]. MXene-based composites include all-MXene hydrogels, MXene/graphene oxide networks, MXene/polymer hydrogels, ionogels, organohydrogels, and multifunctional hybrid systems. Their selection depends on requirements for conductivity, processability, adhesion, stimulus responsiveness, biocompatibility, and controlled degradation [4, 110].

245: 3.1 MXene–Hydrogel Architectures
246: All-MXene hydrogels enable high active-material loading through concentration-induced gelation, ion-mediated assembly, or solvent exchange. Their continuous conductive networks support efficient electron transport, although nanosheet restacking, oxidation, and weak structural integrity remain significant limitations. Ionogel and organogel strategies improve solvent retention and potentially broaden electrochemical operating windows but introduce additional considerations concerning viscosity, cost, and biocompatibility [102, 112, 113]. MXene/graphene oxide (GO) and MXene/reduced graphene oxide (rGO) hydrogels exploit complementary nanosheet properties. Hydrophilic GO promotes aqueous dispersion and hydrogen bonding, whereas rGO enhances electrical conductivity and mechanical reinforcement. Hybrid frameworks suppress MXene restacking, improve ionic accessibility, and may reduce oxidation through controlled interfacial organization [42, 44, 116]. MXene/polymer hydrogels provide greater compositional versatility. Polyacrylamide and poly(acrylic acid) facilitate swelling and functionalization, poly(vinyl alcohol) improves toughness, poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) provides thermoresponsiveness, gelatin and polysaccharides improve biological compatibility, and PEDOT enhances electronic conductivity. However, excessive polymer incorporation can reduce active-material utilization and volumetric capacitance [4, 106, 114, 117].

257: 3.2 Hydrogel Architectures Incorporating MBenes and Other 2D Materials
258: Fabrication must specify electrode, electrolyte, and reinforcement roles. Boride electrodes with separate PVA/H₂SO₄ gel [64] and MBene colloidal electrolytes [38] cannot be treated as identical polymer composites. MXene@PPy/rGO assembly [71], MXene/rGO gelation [35], MoS₂ ionic gelation [36], and MoS₂-assisted polymerization [66] provide distinct processing routes. Transfer requires controls for precursor stability, dispersion, initiator compatibility, washing, and post-gelation sheet identity. Polymer-only and filler-only controls should establish whether improved connectivity compromises separator insulation.
259: 3.3 Interfacial Engineering, Dispersion, and Network Formation
260: Interfacial interactions between MXene nanosheets and polymer chains critically influence electrical percolation, mechanical reinforcement, and fatigue resistance in conductive hydrogels. Hydrogen bonding between MXene surface terminations and polymeric amide, hydroxyl, carboxyl, or sulfonate groups strengthens interfacial compatibility, while multivalent-ion coordination and dynamic covalent interactions enhance structural integrity and self-healing. Conductive polymers further bridge neighboring MXene sheets, improving electronic continuity and processability [24, 106, 120]. Three-dimensional network architectures can be engineered through directional freezing, shear alignment, extrusion printing, magnetic assistance, or ultrasonic alignment. Unlike approximately isotropic random networks, oriented structures provide direction-dependent electrical conduction, ionic transport, and mechanical reinforcement. However, excessive nanosheet alignment may restrict cross-plane transport, necessitating optimization of structural anisotropy [39, 45].
261: Crosslinking density governs the balance between mechanical stability and ionic mobility. Highly cross-linked networks exhibit improved stiffness, dimensional stability, and swelling resistance but may restrict ion diffusion and self-healing. Conversely, loosely cross-linked hydrogels provide greater stretchability and electrolyte uptake while remaining susceptible to creep, dehydration, and structural deformation. Dual-network, interpenetrating-network, and reversible crosslinking strategies integrate load-bearing and energy-dissipating mechanisms. MXene nanosheets can additionally function as conductive reinforcements and dynamic crosslinking sites, coupling mechanical and electrochemical performance [25, 32, 108]. Quantitative control of crosslinking density is therefore essential for optimizing competing material properties. Sacrificial bonds and reversible coordination interactions provide additional energy-dissipation pathways without permanently disrupting conductive networks, enabling improved fatigue resistance and mechanical recovery [24, 25, 32].
262: Fig. 9 illustrates how hierarchical structural design, interfacial coordination, and dynamic crosslinking improve hydrogel conductivity, mechanical durability, self-healing, and multifunctionality. The MXene–tannic acid–polyacrylamide (MTP) hydrogel electrolyte was fabricated through one-pot thermal polymerization. Initially, ZnSO₄·7H₂O was dissolved in 8 mL of MXene–tannic acid (MT) dispersion to obtain a 2 M electrolyte. Subsequently, 1.2 g acrylamide (AM), 4 mg N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide (MBA), and 40 mg ammonium persulfate (APS) were added. Following degassing, polymerization proceeded at 60 °C for 2 h. MXene loadings of 10, 24, 50, 80, and 100 mg produced MTP-1 to MTP-5, respectively. Pure PAM, MXene–PAM (MP), and tannic acid–PAM (TP) controls were prepared similarly [121].
263: For device assembly, Zn²⁺-preintercalated V₂O₅ (Z-VO) was synthesized by dispersing 0.364 g V₂O₅ in 30 mL water, adding 2 mL H₂O₂ (30 wt.%), and subsequently introducing 0.75 mmol ZnSO₄. Hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 24 h produced Z-VO, which was mixed with acetylene black and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) at a 7:2:1 mass ratio in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). The slurry was deposited onto titanium foil and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for approximately 12 h, yielding an active-material loading of approximately 3.0 mg cm⁻². Fig. 9(a) presents the spider-web-inspired MTP architecture, comprising a three-dimensional PAM framework reinforced by tannic acid-functionalized MXene. The interconnected network provides Zn²⁺ adsorption sites and transport channels. As illustrated in Fig. 9(b), conventional aqueous ZnSO₄ electrolytes can promote dendritic deposition, hydrogen evolution, and concentration polarization. Although PAM electrolytes partially immobilize free water, limited interfacial contact and sluggish Zn²⁺ transport may persist. In contrast, MXene surface groups and tannic acid phenolic functionalities regulate Zn²⁺ migration and water coordination, suppressing parasitic reactions and promoting more uniform zinc deposition. Conductive hydrogels are increasingly investigated for neuronal interfaces, physiological monitoring, and bioelectronic stimulation [122, 123]. Conventional polymer hydrogels generally exhibit insufficient electronic conductivity compared with conductive polymers, carbon materials, and metals [124-126].

270: 3.4 Functional, Self-Healing, and Degradable Composite Hydrogels
271: Self-healing is essential for hydrogel–MXene energy-storage systems because mechanical damage disrupts electronic and ionic transport pathways. Effective recovery requires the reconstruction of polymer–polymer bonds, polymer–MXene interactions, and nanosheet contacts. Healing kinetics depend on polymer-chain mobility, hydration, temperature, and reversible-bond dynamics. Near-infrared (NIR)-responsive MXenes can accelerate network reconstruction through localized photothermal heating, although excessive heating may induce dehydration or tissue damage [31, 108, 134]. Self-healing performance should be assessed through both mechanical and electrochemical recovery. Visual reconnection or tensile-strength restoration does not necessarily indicate recovered conductivity or capacitance. Accordingly, healing efficiency should consider mechanical strength, elongation, electrical resistance, impedance, capacitance retention, and electrolyte leakage before damage, after damage, and following recovery. Experimental conditions, including healing duration, contact pressure, temperature, hydration, and external stimulation, should also be documented [108, 134, 135].

285: 4. Design Requirements for Soft Energy Storage
286: Soft mechatronic energy-storage devices must satisfy interconnected material, device, and system requirements. Essential material properties include conductivity, mechanical compliance, water retention, biocompatibility, and chemical stability. Device performance depends on capacitance, operating voltage, energy and power density, cycling durability, encapsulation, and deformation tolerance. System integration further requires compatibility with sensors, actuators, energy harvesters, wireless modules, and power-management circuits [5, 16, 20]. Conventional electrochemical measurements must be complemented by mechanical and environmental testing under bending, twisting, stretching, compression, perspiration, dehydration, temperature cycling, and repeated adhesion. Robotic applications require actuation-cycle evaluation, whereas biomedical systems additionally demand saline resistance, cytocompatibility, sterilization tolerance, and degradation assessments [2, 13, 17]. Testing sequences should distinguish residual damage from dynamic performance changes. Mechanical cycling followed by electrochemical characterization identifies permanent degradation, while simultaneous testing reveals operational instability. Dehydration–rehydration and repeated attachment–detachment experiments further assess environmental recovery and adhesive fatigue, providing realistic service-life estimates [2, 13, 17].
287: 4.1 Mechanical Compliance Under Deformation
288: Mechanical compliance requires sustained flexibility, fatigue resistance, and electrochemical stability rather than high single-cycle stretchability. Device durability depends on the entire multilayer structure, including electrodes, electrolytes, current collectors, separators, encapsulation, adhesives, and interconnects, because localized failure can compromise overall functionality [17, 25, 123]. Bending performance depends on device thickness, curvature, strain distribution, and mechanical cycling. Accordingly, characterization should report bending radius, thickness, strain amplitude, cycle number, recovery time, humidity, and electrical retention. Microscopic evaluation of electrode edges and interconnects helps identify localized damage [16, 17]. Complete-device testing is essential because independently flexible components may experience interfacial shear, delamination, or modulus mismatch after assembly. Neutral-axis optimization, compliant interconnects, and suitable encapsulation can mitigate these effects [16, 17, 123]. Compression may redistribute ions and expel water, whereas torsion can disrupt electrode–electrolyte contact. Dynamic crosslinks, gradient-modulus structures, kirigami designs, serpentine interconnects, and adhesive encapsulation improve deformation tolerance [45, 108].

311: 4.3 Encapsulation, Environmental Stability, and System Integration
312: Encapsulation is essential for hydrogel–MXene energy-storage devices because it regulates water retention, oxygen penetration, electrolyte leakage, mechanical compliance, and operational stability. Suitable encapsulation materials include elastomers, biodegradable polymers, ion-selective membranes, and multilayer barriers, selected according to device functionality and intended lifetime. For biodegradable systems, encapsulation must additionally permit controlled water penetration after the designated service period [13, 14, 137]. Encapsulation should function as an active structural component rather than passive packaging. Although impermeable barriers improve hydration retention, they may restrict programmed degradation, whereas permeable coatings can compromise shelf life. Effective MXene protection requires comprehensive edge sealing, interconnect isolation, and minimization of processing-induced defects [13, 14, 30].
313: MXene oxidation remains a major limitation because it depends on oxygen exposure, hydration, pH, temperature, nanosheet dimensions, defects, and surface terminations. Polymer wrapping, antioxidant additives, graphene hybridization, oxygen-barrier coatings, controlled storage temperature, and pH regulation can mitigate oxidation while preserving ionic accessibility [30, 41, 45]. System-level integration requires reliable electrical and mechanical coupling between energy-storage units and functional components. Printed MXene electrodes can be integrated with wireless coils, sensors, photodetectors, and triboelectric nanogenerators. However, autonomous operation additionally requires rectification, voltage regulation, charge balancing, and protection circuits to ensure stable energy delivery [18, 20, 40].

322: 4.4 Material-Specific Trade-Offs: Conductivity, Oxidation, Processability, and Safety
323: Conductivity, stability, and exposure must be assessed together. MXene/PVA electrolytes [33] require electronic isolation, whereas MXene/PPy electrodes [34] require persistent conducting contacts. Porous MXene/PNIPAAm aging [104] and MBene colloidal electrolytes [38] demonstrate formulation-specific stability questions. 324: 5. Hydrogel-Based Flexible Energy Storage Devices
325: Hydrogel nanomaterial-based energy-storage devices integrate mechanical flexibility with electrochemical functionality through multifunctional polymer networks. Hydrogels can function as leakage-resistant electrolytes, conductive scaffolds incorporating MXenes, graphene derivatives, conductive polymers, or redox-active particles, and integrated components providing separation, adhesion, sensing, and encapsulation. Such multifunctionality reduces interfacial complexity and improves mechanical compatibility in soft mechatronic systems [1, 4, 5]. Design requirements vary with device architecture. Supercapacitors primarily require accessible electrochemically active surfaces and stable voltage windows, whereas batteries demand suppression of dendrite formation, electrode-volume changes, and parasitic reactions. Microsupercapacitors prioritize miniaturization, printing resolution, and interconnect reliability, while self-powered and biomedical systems require efficient energy management, safety, and predictable operational lifetimes. Accordingly, hydrogel–MXene architectures should be optimized according to application-specific requirements [16, 20, 115].
326: 5.1 Flexible Supercapacitors and Microsupercapacitors
327: Flexible supercapacitors are particularly suitable for soft mechatronic applications because of their high power density, rapid charge–discharge capability, and cycling durability. MXene electrodes provide high volumetric capacitance, hydrogel electrolytes maintain deformable electrode–electrolyte interfaces, and flexible polymeric or textile substrates provide mechanical support. However, sustained electrical contact and ionic accessibility during repeated deformation remain essential challenges [5, 39, 144]. Practical device demonstrations should extend beyond powering light-emitting diodes because such tests provide limited information about real-world operating requirements. More representative evaluations include powering wearable strain sensors during movement, buffering photovoltaic or triboelectric energy, supporting intermittent wireless transmission, and supplying soft actuators during cyclic operation. These demonstrations establish a clearer relationship between electrochemical properties and functional autonomy [18, 20, 40].
328: Microsupercapacitors (MSCs) provide additional advantages through miniaturized interdigitated electrode configurations that shorten ionic diffusion pathways and facilitate integration with sensors and electronic circuits. Inkjet printing, screen printing, extrusion printing, transfer printing, stamping, and laser patterning enable controlled MXene deposition onto flexible substrates. However, electrode quality strongly depends on ink viscosity, surface tension, dispersion stability, drying behavior, and post-deposition conductivity [19, 39, 109]. Additive-free MXene inks offer high active-material fractions but remain susceptible to oxidation, nozzle clogging, nonuniform drying, and insufficient substrate adhesion. Incorporating polymeric or hydrogel additives improves rheological properties and processability, although excessive loading may reduce electrical conductivity. Consequently, systematic characterization should consider ink rheology, nanosheet stability, printing resolution, and electrochemical performance [20, 131-133]. Electrical integration is equally important for practical MSC applications. Series-connected cells increase output voltage but introduce potential cell mismatch, whereas parallel configurations increase capacity and current delivery. Conductive connections employing liquid metals, printed MXene, serpentine conductors, or elastomer-supported metallic films must maintain low resistance during repeated deformation [19, 145, 146].

397: 5.2 Flexible Batteries and Hybrid Energy Systems
398: Flexible batteries and hybrid energy-storage systems are essential for autonomous soft mechatronic platforms requiring sustained energy delivery, mechanical compliance, and efficient power management. Hydrogel electrolytes improve battery safety, suppress leakage, and maintain electrode contact during deformation, while MXene-based microsupercapacitors (MSCs) buffer intermittent harvested energy and supply rapid power pulses for sensing, wireless communication, and actuation [1, 5, 18-20].
399: Aqueous zinc-ion batteries (ZIBs) are particularly attractive because of their relatively low cost, safety, and compatibility with hydrogel electrolytes. Nevertheless, zinc dendrite formation, hydrogen evolution, cathode dissolution, and electrolyte dehydration compromise cycling stability [1, 125]. Other battery chemistries, including lithium-, sodium-, and multivalent-ion systems, may experience passivation, corrosion, gas evolution, and undesirable polymer–electrolyte interactions. Although MXene incorporation improves conductivity, ion transport, and interfacial stability, uncontrolled surface reactions can accelerate degradation. Consequently, flexible batteries require electrochemical and mechanical reliability assessments under realistic operating conditions [123, 125, 143, 157]. MXenes function as conductive hosts, protective interlayers, current collectors, surface coatings, and ion-storage electrodes. Their hydrophilic surfaces and tunable interlayer galleries facilitate ionic transport, particularly for strongly solvated multivalent ions [158, 159]. Hydrogel separators additionally prevent direct electrode contact while maintaining ionic conductivity, puncture resistance, and dimensional stability. Self-healing and antifreezing formulations further improve environmental tolerance [1, 115].
400: Hybrid architectures combine complementary energy technologies. Triboelectric nanogenerators harvest mechanical motion, photovoltaic cells convert light, and thermoelectric devices utilize temperature gradients, whereas batteries provide sustained energy and supercapacitors deliver rapid power [18, 19, 160]. MXenes can serve as electrodes, interconnects, electromagnetic shields, and sensors, while hydrogels provide ionic conduction, adhesion, and compliant interfaces [3, 19, 20]. Effective integration requires quantitative evaluation of harvested energy, rectification efficiency, storage capacity, leakage, self-discharge, and load requirements. Operational demonstrations should therefore assess realistic duty cycles rather than brief device activation [18, 20]. Fig. 13 presents flexible microbatteries, interconnected MSC arrays, and integrated energy-harvesting systems, emphasizing voltage matching, mechanical reliability, and coordinated energy distribution [18-20, 160].

409: 5.3 Device-Level Comparison of Different 2D Material–Hydrogel Systems
410: Device comparisons must include both electrodes, electrolyte, collectors, and packaging. Boride interdigitated devices [64], LDH/carbon-hydrogel capacitors [65], MoS₂ membranes [36], rGO/polyacrylate cells [70], and MXene/rGO hybrid capacitors [35] use different reactions and component roles; MXene/PVA electrolytes add another configuration [33]. Report delivered discharge energy, footprint, thickness, loading, voltage, and mass basis. Compare deformation at matched strain or radius, hydration, and cycling conditions, including impedance and self-discharge. Battery capacity and capacitor capacitance must remain distinct.
411: 6. Soft Robotics and Self-Powered Wearables
412: Soft robotics and wearable electronics require energy-storage systems that maintain electrochemical functionality under continuous mechanical deformation. Conventional rigid batteries restrict movement and concentrate mechanical stress, whereas liquid electrolytes present leakage and packaging challenges. Hydrogel–MXene composites offer flexible, stretchable, adhesive, and multifunctional architectures, enabling distributed energy storage across robotic structures, wearable textiles, and conformable electronic interfaces [2, 3, 106]. Energy consumption varies considerably among soft mechatronic components. Sensors generally require low continuous power, wireless communication modules demand intermittent energy pulses, and actuators may require short-duration high-current outputs. Hybrid architectures combining flexible batteries with locally integrated microsupercapacitors can therefore provide sustained energy storage and rapid power delivery. Patterned hydrogel–MXene electrodes additionally facilitate distributed integration without substantially increasing mechanical stiffness, supporting compact and mechanically adaptive electronic systems [16, 20].
413: 6.1 Energy Storage for Soft Robotic Actuation
414: Energy-storage placement critically influences robotic deformation, mechanical stability, and actuation efficiency. Positioning storage devices near the neutral mechanical plane minimizes bending-induced strain, whereas embedding conductive layers reduces external wiring but may alter stiffness distribution. Gradient hydrogels and patterned MXene electrodes enable localized energy storage in mechanically favorable regions while maintaining flexibility and actuation amplitude [45, 108]. Different actuation mechanisms impose distinct electrical and thermal requirements. Pneumatic systems require pumps and valves, dielectric elastomers demand high operating voltages, thermoresponsive hydrogels require heating, and electrochemical actuators depend on ionic transport. MXene-containing hydrogels additionally support photothermal and Joule-heating actuation through their optical absorption and conductive networks. However, thermal regulation, electrical isolation, and stable electrode interfaces remain essential for reliable multifunctional operation [106, 108]. Mechanical durability is equally important because robotic components experience repeated compression, abrasion, puncturing, and localized damage. Self-healing hydrogel–MXene networks can reconstruct conductive pathways, while adhesive interfaces minimize delamination. Healing performance should also be evaluated following electrochemical cycling because interfacial reactions and electrolyte changes may influence the reversible interactions responsible for structural recovery [108, 134].
415: Fig. 14 demonstrates the integration of flexible energy storage, distributed sensing, and electrochemical monitoring within mechanically adaptive platforms. These applications require coordinated optimization of mechanical compliance, electrode–electrolyte contact, electrical stability, and energy delivery. Consequently, storage placement and material architecture must balance deformation tolerance with the power requirements of integrated sensing and actuation systems [16, 20, 45, 106, 108]. The mechanical compliance and sensing characteristics of PSC hydrogels enabled the development of self-powered strain sensors integrated with flexible batteries. Fig. 14(a) illustrates their conformable attachment to different body regions. Fig. 14(b) presents reproducible resistance responses during finger bending between 0° and 90°, demonstrating angle-dependent sensitivity and stable signal generation. Real-time resistance variations during finger movement are further demonstrated in Fig. 14(c). Beyond large joint movements, the sensor detected subtle physiological activities, including swallowing and throat vibrations during pronunciation of “time,” “test,” “wide,” “temperature,” “battery,” and “sensor,” as illustrated in Fig. 14(d). The integrated Zn∥PSC∥V₂O₅ battery retained approximately 93% capacity following bending while maintaining stable Coulombic efficiency. Puncture and compression tests additionally demonstrated improved operational safety by preventing electrode contact and internal short circuits, unlike liquid-electrolyte batteries that exhibited leakage and voltage deterioration [171]. PSC hydrogels were further employed to monitor internal battery deformation associated with reversible zinc deposition and stripping. Fig. 14(e) illustrates the experimental configuration, incorporating a hydrogel sensing component, electrochemical workstation, source measurement unit, and X-ray-transparent sample holder. The sensor was positioned alongside the energy-storage unit to measure resistance variations induced by internal pressure changes. Simultaneously, transmission-mode operando X-ray diffraction (XRD) monitored electrode structural evolution during electrochemical cycling, enabling correlation between mechanical responses and zinc deposition behavior [171].
416: The corresponding operando XRD results in Fig. 14(f) revealed minimal structural changes in the V₂O₅ cathode, with lattice-parameter variations below 0.8%. No characteristic ZnₓV₂O₅·nH₂O reflection appeared within the 3°–5° region, while the V₂O₅ (200) and (110) reflections remained near 7.2° and 12.2°, respectively. In contrast, the Zn (002) reflection at approximately 16.5° gradually weakened during stripping and recovered during subsequent plating, confirming reversible zinc deposition. The synchronized PSC sensor exhibited a maximum relative resistance variation of approximately 0.19%. Increasing resistance during charging reflected deformation induced by zinc deposition and associated changes in ionic transport distances. This coupled electrochemical–mechanical response enabled real-time monitoring of internal pressure fluctuations and electrode-volume evolution [171]. Self-powered pressure-sensing matrices provide additional capabilities for spatially resolved mechanical monitoring. Fig. 14(g) illustrates a flexible Zn–MnO₂ battery-based sensor comprising orthogonally patterned electrode layers and an ionic gel-paper separator. The integrated 16 × 16-pixel sensing array incorporates a column-selection multiplexer, analog-to-digital converter, digital-to-analog converter, and field-programmable gate array. These components enable coordinated pixel addressing, signal acquisition, and reconstruction of pressure distributions, supporting applications in wearable electronics, robotics, and physiological monitoring [172].
417: The dynamic sensing characteristics are demonstrated in Fig. 14(h), including distinguishable responses during single- and double-click mouse operations, simulated tremor monitoring, and human gait detection. Mechanical stimulation at 6 Hz generated recognizable signals relevant to simulated Parkinsonian tremors, while shoe-insole integration enabled differentiation among walking, brisk walking, running, and jumping through variations in signal amplitude and frequency. Robotic fingertip integration was also considered for remote monitoring in challenging environments. Fig. 14(i) further presents a flexible visual touch device combining a paper-based battery, pressure sensor, and light-emitting diode. The device remained functional when wrapped around a cylindrical surface, with illumination intensity varying according to applied pressure [172].
418: Hydrogel electrolyte engineering additionally improves zinc-based energy-storage reliability by regulating water activity and ion transport. Molecular simulations indicated that multicomponent polymer networks establish extensive hydrogen-bonding interactions with water, modifying Zn²⁺ solvation and suppressing undesirable interfacial reactions [173]. Fig. 14(j) compares Zn//Zn symmetric cells containing PAM, PAM/LA, and PAM/LA/PSBMA hydrogel electrolytes. At 10 mA cm⁻² and 1 mAh cm⁻², the respective cycling lifetimes were approximately 10, 74, and 290 h, with the optimized electrolyte exhibiting an average zinc depth of discharge of 1.7%. Fig. 14(k) presents the corresponding zinc-anode morphologies following cycling. The PAM electrolyte produced pronounced dendritic deposits, whereas PAM/LA reduced dendrite formation and interfacial degradation. The PAM/LA/PSBMA electrolyte generated a comparatively uniform zinc surface with limited fine dendritic structures. At a lower current density of 1 mA cm⁻² and an areal capacity of 2 mAh cm⁻², the optimized symmetric cell maintained stable cycling for 681 h, demonstrating improved zinc-deposition reversibility and interfacial stability [173].

424: 6.2 Bioinspired Energy Storage Architectures
425: Bioinspired architectures provide effective strategies for improving electrochemical performance through structural optimization rather than chemical modification alone. Biological systems offer hierarchical designs that integrate mechanical flexibility, efficient transport, and structural durability. Skin-inspired multilayers provide compliant interfaces and protected conductive regions, muscle-like aligned fibers facilitate anisotropic deformation and mechanical reinforcement, vascular networks support distributed ionic transport, and leaf-inspired porous architectures enhance interfacial accessibility. These principles can be incorporated into hydrogel–MXene composites through layered casting, directional freezing, three-dimensional printing, microfluidic processing, and gradient filler distribution [174-177]. Biomorphic architectures are particularly advantageous for energy-storage devices subjected to repetitive deformation. Aligned MXene networks promote directional electronic conductivity, while compliant polymer regions accommodate mechanical strain and dissipate energy. Vascular-like electrolyte channels facilitate ionic migration within thicker structures, whereas epidermal multilayers protect electrochemically active components from moisture, perspiration, and biological interfaces. Such hierarchical organization enables improved coordination of conductivity, mechanical toughness, and biocompatibility compared with homogeneous composite networks [45, 106].
426: Nevertheless, reproducible fabrication remains challenging because structural characteristics depend strongly on processing conditions. Variations in freezing rate, solvent composition, printing speed, ink viscosity, and MXene concentration can influence pore dimensions, nanosheet orientation, and interfacial integrity. Consequently, scalable manufacturing methods, including roll-to-roll printing, digital light processing, direct ink writing, and continuous coating, require standardized characterization of pore-size distribution, orientation factors, and electrical percolation thresholds to ensure consistent device performance [20, 39].

431: Fig. 15 illustrates how bioinspired structural gradients, reversible cross-linking, and interfacial adhesion contribute to the mechanical and electrochemical stability of flexible hydrogel electrolytes. These strategies address coupled transport and mechanical limitations through controlled network architecture rather than relying exclusively on increased conductive filler loading [39, 174-177]. Cartilage-inspired gradient hydrogels provide a representative approach to stabilizing zinc-metal anodes. Natural articular cartilage possesses a hierarchical structure in which densely packed collagen fibers and proteoglycans near the articular surface absorb mechanical impacts, whereas relatively loose regions near the subchondral bone facilitate nutrient transport and cellular metabolism. This spatial organization combines mechanical protection with efficient mass transport. Inspired by these characteristics, gradient hydrogel electrolytes incorporating poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and cellulose nanofibers (CNFs) were developed to improve Zn²⁺ transport and interfacial stability. Their abundant zincophilic hydroxyl groups additionally support network reinforcement and interactions with zinc ions, as illustrated in Fig. 15(a) [178].
432: The electrochemical applicability of the PCG20–PC5 gradient electrolyte was evaluated in flexible Zn–MnO₂ batteries. Fig. 15(b) presents capacity retention following charging to 1.8 V, resting for 72 h, and discharging to 1 V, providing evidence of charge-storage stability. Fig. 15(c) demonstrates capacity retention under different bending angles, with only minor variations in electrochemical performance. These findings indicate that gradient-network organization can maintain ionic transport and electrode contact during deformation, supporting flexible energy-storage applications [178]. Dynamic polymer interactions offer complementary advantages for self-healing gel polymer electrolytes (GPEs). Fig. 15(d) illustrates the DA-Alg/PAAm-O network and its mechanical recovery behavior. The separated electrolyte could reconnect and subsequently withstand stretching while supporting a 200 g load. Tensile stress–strain measurements in Fig. 15(e) demonstrate a healing efficiency of 99.9% after the first healing cycle and 72.4% after five cycles, compared with 73.3% for PAAm-O after five cycles. Pristine PAAm exhibited negligible recovery. This behavior was attributed primarily to reversible hydrophobic associations that promote polymer-network reconstruction following damage [179]. Interfacial adhesion further determines electrolyte reliability during mechanical deformation. As presented in Fig. 15(f), DA-Alg/PAAm-O exhibited adhesion strengths of approximately 30–80 kPa on carbon cloth, carbon paper, zinc foil, and nickel foam, exceeding the 10–28 kPa recorded for PAAm-O. Catechol functionalities within DA-Alg and hydrophobic interactions between polymer chains and substrates contributed to stronger interfacial bonding. Fig. 15(g) demonstrates improved adhesion to electrode substrates, whereas the weaker PAAm-O interfaces were more susceptible to separation. These characteristics help maintain electrochemically active contact during bending [179].
433: The electrochemical consequences of interfacial engineering were investigated through zinc plating and stripping. Fig. 15(h) and (i) compare the behavior of DA-Alg/PAAm-O and PAAm-O electrolytes, respectively. Stronger adhesion in the modified network reduces interfacial displacement and supports more uniform zinc deposition. Fig. 15(j) further demonstrates that DA-Alg/PAAm-O-based Zn//Zn symmetric cells maintained stable cycling beyond 1000 h at a bending radius of 2 mm, current density of 1 mA cm⁻², and areal capacity of 0.2 mAh cm⁻². In comparison, PAAm-O-based cells operated for approximately 400 h under identical conditions, highlighting the importance of interfacial stability [179]. Self-healing behavior was further characterized through repeated mechanical damage. Fig. 15(k) presents optical microscopy observations showing progressive crack closure and restoration of structural continuity within 24 h. Reversible hydrophobic associations among OMA segments and hydrogen bonding involving DA functional groups facilitated this reconstruction. Fig. 15(l) demonstrates approximately 90.1% ionic-conductivity retention after 20 cutting–healing cycles, with remaining losses primarily associated with dehydration. Importantly, Fig. 15(m) shows that assembled aqueous zinc-ion batteries maintained approximately 94.4% capacity-based healing efficiency after 20 cycles, accompanied by nearly overlapping charge–discharge profiles. These results demonstrate simultaneous mechanical and electrochemical recovery [179]. Collectively, bioinspired gradient architectures, dynamic bonding, and reinforced electrode–electrolyte interfaces provide complementary approaches to improving mechanical adaptability, ionic transport, and long-term electrochemical stability. Their integration offers a promising foundation for durable, self-healing, and conformable energy-storage systems suitable for adaptive soft mechatronic devices.
434: Recent advances in bio-inspired and multifunctional material engineering provide valuable strategies for developing next-generation flexible energy systems. Wang et al. [180] demonstrated how bio-inspired self-assembly and ordered interfacial architectures can improve the mechanical robustness and ionic transport of composite solid electrolytes. Similarly, Song et al. [181] highlighted biomimetic structural, surface, and interfacial engineering approaches to overcome electrode instability, electrolyte corrosion, and limited cycling durability in zinc-based energy storage devices. Extending these concepts beyond energy storage, Afzal et al. [105] reviewed the multifunctional integration of two-dimensional materials, including MXenes, graphene, and transition-metal dichalcogenides, into triboelectric nanogenerators for enhanced energy harvesting and self-powered sensing. Collectively, these studies emphasize the importance of hierarchical structural design, interfacial optimization, and multifunctional material integration for advancing mechanically adaptable and self-powered energy technologies
435: 6.3 Wearable Sensing and Self-Powered Interfaces
436: Hydrogel–MXene composites offer considerable potential for wearable sensing because they combine mechanical conformability, electrical conductivity, and efficient signal transduction. Applied strain and pressure modify conductive pathways between MXene nanosheets, producing measurable resistance or capacitance variations [182, 183]. Temperature influences polymer-chain mobility and ionic transport, while hydrated interfaces facilitate physiological signal acquisition. Integrating local microsupercapacitors further enables intermittent sensing and communication without bulky external power sources [2, 3, 107]. Multifunctional wearable devices increasingly integrate energy storage, sensing, and adhesion within shared conductive networks. Although this approach reduces thickness and improves conformability, it introduces interactions between electrochemical and sensing functions. Mechanical deformation may alter capacitance, while charging and discharging can influence sensing signals through ionic redistribution or Joule heating. Therefore, reliable operation requires optimized device architecture, signal calibration, and power-management circuitry [3, 107, 184].
437: Electronic skin requires distributed sensing, mechanical softness, and stable responses during repeated movement. MXene-based hydrogels provide adjustable sensitivity and deformation tolerance, although highly sensitive percolation networks may exhibit restricted sensing ranges. Microstructured surfaces, multilayer architectures, and mixed-dimensional conductive networks help balance sensitivity, stretchability, and operational stability [107, 136, 184]. Smart textiles introduce additional requirements, including breathability, washability, abrasion resistance, and compatibility with conventional textile manufacturing. MXene coatings enable conductive fiber integration, whereas hydrogels improve interfacial contact. However, dehydration and laundering can compromise water-rich networks, necessitating encapsulation or organohydrogel and ionogel formulations. Performance assessment should consequently include repeated folding, friction, washing, and humidity cycling [39, 185]. For human–machine interfaces, reproducible gesture recognition is essential for interpreting finger bending, wrist motion, swallowing, speech, handwriting, and rehabilitation movements. Hydrogel–MXene sensors integrated with local energy storage provide lightweight sensing platforms, although hydration-dependent drift and mechanical fatigue require appropriate calibration [135, 184].

Hydration, perspiration, adhesion fatigue, and environmental exposure must also be considered when assessing long-term performance [2, 3, 107, 135, 184, 185]. 455: 7. Biomedical and Biodegradable Energy Storage
456: Biomedical energy-storage systems require stringent material safety, mechanical compatibility, and operational reliability because they may directly contact skin, mucosal surfaces, wounds, or internal tissues. Hydrogels are particularly attractive because their hydrated polymer networks resemble biological tissues and minimize mechanical mismatch. However, comprehensive biocompatibility evaluation must consider all device constituents, including residual monomers, cross-linkers, initiators, electrolytic salts, solvents, MXene nanosheets, oxidation products, encapsulation materials, adhesives, and degradation byproducts [2, 13, 111]. Unlike conventional wearable electronics, biomedical devices prioritize biological safety, predictable operational lifetime, sterilization compatibility, and electrochemical stability in physiological environments. Transient devices may undergo controlled degradation following therapeutic intervention or wound monitoring, whereas implantable systems may require wireless charging or energy harvesting to minimize surgical replacement. Consequently, material selection must balance electrochemical performance with biological compatibility and programmed device degradation [14, 15, 137].
457: 7.1 Biocompatible Hydrogels for Bioelectronics
458: Biocompatibility assessment requires evaluation beyond conventional in vitro cell-viability measurements. Although cellular assays provide preliminary information, they cannot adequately characterize immune responses, protein adsorption, chronic inflammation, or mechanical irritation. Epidermal devices require testing for skin irritation, perspiration exposure, adhesive removal, and microbial contamination, whereas mucosal and implantable interfaces demand additional evaluation of saline swelling, enzymatic degradation, sterilization resistance, and prolonged material leaching [13, 193, 194]. Biological compatibility also depends on exposure duration and physiological conditions. Materials suitable for temporary skin contact may exhibit adverse responses during prolonged implantation because hydration, pH, ionic strength, enzymatic activity, and mechanical motion influence chemical stability and constituent release. For MXene-containing systems, nanosheet release, oxidation products, residual etchants, and photothermal heating require careful evaluation alongside polymer degradation. These considerations distinguish short-term wearable comfort from long-term biomedical safety [13, 193, 194].
459: MXene concentration, flake dimensions, oxidation state, surface terminations, and exposure duration further influence biological interactions. Polymer functionalization and encapsulation can restrict direct tissue exposure while retaining conductivity, although excessive encapsulation may compromise ionic transport or sensing sensitivity. Consequently, biomedical devices should identify whether MXene nanosheets remain exposed, partially embedded, or completely isolated within the polymer network [111, 195]. For electrophysiological applications, hydrated interfaces reduce contact impedance and movement-related artifacts. Adhesive hydrogels preserve conformal contact during physiological motion, while MXene conductive networks support electrical signal acquisition. When energy storage and sensing are integrated within the same biomedical platform, leakage currents, localized heating, and unintended electrochemical reactions must be controlled through appropriate insulation, current limitation, and device-level isolation [2, 193].
460: 7.2 Biodegradable Device Components and Encapsulation
461: Biodegradable energy-storage systems require coordinated control of operational stability, environmental protection, and programmed degradation. During service, electrodes, electrolytes, and encapsulation layers must maintain electrical continuity, ionic transport, and mechanical integrity. Following the intended operating period, degradation may be initiated through water penetration, hydrolysis, oxidation, enzymatic activity, or pH changes. Encapsulation therefore regulates the transition from stable operation to controlled material disintegration, preventing premature failure while enabling the intended transient functionality [14, 137, 142]. Hydrogel electrolytes offer considerable potential for biodegradable devices because natural and hydrolyzable synthetic polymers can undergo controlled degradation. However, electrode materials and current collectors introduce additional challenges. Carbon-based conductors may remain environmentally persistent, transient metals release ionic species during dissolution, and MXene nanosheets may undergo oxidation or fragmentation, with their long-term biological and environmental fate remaining insufficiently established [14, 15].
462: Sustainable fabrication also requires minimizing hazardous processing chemicals, energy consumption, and material waste. Water-based inks, alternative etching approaches, low-temperature processing, recyclable substrates, and life-cycle assessment are therefore important considerations for environmentally responsible hydrogel–MXene devices [58, 137, 196, 197]. End-of-life requirements depend on application. Disposable wearable systems prioritize environmental compatibility and safe disposal, implantable devices require controlled degradation and biological clearance, and soft robotic systems may benefit more from repairability and modular replacement. Accordingly, sustainability assessment should consider fabrication hazards, operational lifetime, reusability, degradation kinetics, and residual material composition [14, 15, 137].

Implantable systems encounter saline, proteins, enzymes, cells, and motion, which can alter hydrogel swelling, ionic exchange, MXene oxidation, protein adsorption, and encapsulation integrity. 478: 7.3 Biocompatibility and Degradation Considerations for 2D Fillers
479: Polymer dissolution, filler transformation, and biological clearance are different endpoints. MXene/PVA/bacterial-cellulose degradation [138], functionalized MoS₂ wound-repair studies [202], and multisensing MoS₂ hydrogels [77] provide formulation-specific evidence. Mg–Mo₂C transient batteries [201], bioresorbable zinc-ion cells [200], and biodegradable separators [199] address different device components. Assessment should identify released species, particulate residue, exposure dose, duration, and tissue response after operation and sterilization. 
480: 8. Comparative Analysis and Future Perspectives
481: Comparative assessment of flexible energy-storage systems remains challenging because reported electrochemical performance depends on measurement configuration, normalization methods, and testing conditions. Three-electrode measurements frequently differ from full-device results, while normalization to active materials rather than complete device mass may overestimate practical performance. Inconsistent mechanical testing further complicates comparison [5, 16, 143]. Gou et al. [203] highlighted the role of high-concentration gel electrolytes and polymer-network engineering in enhancing ionic transport, interfacial stability, and electrochemical performance. Their findings provide useful design strategies for developing efficient and stable hydrogel-based energy-storage systems [203]. Standardized reporting should specify electrode configuration, gravimetric, areal, or volumetric normalization, mechanical testing conditions, electrolyte composition, hydrogel water content, MXene loading, flake size, encapsulation, and storage history [5, 16, 115]. 535: 8.1 Comparative Energy-Storage Performance Across Material Families
536: Hydrogel-only systems provide mechanical softness and ionic transport but generally require electroactive components. MXene films offer high conductivity and capacitance but suffer restacking, oxidation, brittleness, and limited stretchability. Meaningful benchmarking requires both component-level and full-device measurements, including encapsulation and electrical interconnections [16, 20, 39]. Compared with carbon aerogels, conductive polymers, metal oxides, and layered double hydroxides, hydrogel–MXene composites offer coupled ionic–electronic transport but remain vulnerable to dehydration and oxidation [31, 41, 115].

540: 8.2 Materials, Safety, Sustainability, Scalability, and Manufacturing Challenges
541: MXene oxidation reduces conductivity, electrochemical accessibility, and reproducibility. Polymer wrapping, antioxidants, and oxygen-barrier encapsulation may improve stability, provided ionic transport remains sufficient [30, 41, 45]. Hydrogel dehydration and freezing also impair mechanical compliance and ionic conductivity. Organohydrogels and ionogels offer alternatives, although solvent safety and cost require evaluation [31, 48, 115]. Manufacturing challenges include hazardous fluoride-based etching, inconsistent nanosheet quality, and unstable printable formulations. Alternative synthesis routes and standardized control of ink viscosity, oxidation, adhesion, and flake size are therefore essential [20, 39, 58, 196, 197, 209]. Sustainability additionally requires evaluating processing impacts, repairability, operational lifetime, and complete-device biodegradation rather than polymer degradation alone [14, 137, 142].

576: 8.3 Research Gaps and Opportunities for MBenes and Other Emerging 2D Materials
577: Emerging-material assessment should connect verified nanosheet identity to gel integration and packaged operation. MoOₓ/MoB anodes [37], MBene colloidal electrolytes [38], MoAl₁₋ₓB electrodes [105], and boride/gel microsupercapacitors [64] establish different stages. 578: 8.4 Future Directions and Conclusions
579: The evidence examined throughout this review suggests that the principal research priority is to shift optimization from individual electrodes and hydrogel formulations toward complete, mechanically compliant power systems. High MXene conductivity and pseudocapacitive activity are beneficial only when ionic pathways, electrode–electrolyte contact, current collection, and encapsulation remain functional during use. Equally, hydrogel softness, adhesion, and self-healing do not ensure device reliability if dehydration or interfacial resistance limits usable energy. Important trade-offs remain: highly porous networks improve ion accessibility but may reduce volumetric packing density, while protective barriers can improve moisture retention at the expense of compliance or controlled degradation. Future materials selection should therefore be guided by the intended duty cycle and coordinated electrochemical, mechanical, and environmental criteria rather than a single peak performance value [4, 5, 16, 31].
580: A first requirement is more reproducible and application-relevant benchmarking. Studies should clearly distinguish three-electrode material measurements from two-electrode and fully packaged device performance and report the normalization basis for capacitance, capacity, energy, and power, including active-material mass, device area, and device volume as applicable. MXene loading, flake dimensions, electrolyte composition, hydrogel water content, electrode thickness, encapsulation, and storage history should accompany these metrics. Mechanical protocols should specify bending radius or applied strain, loading mode and frequency, cycle number, recovery interval, and test environment; capacitance or capacity retention, impedance, leakage, and self-discharge should be measured before, during, and after deformation when feasible. Such reporting would distinguish apparent electrochemical durability from actual operational stability and make comparisons among hydrogel-only, MXene-rich, and hybrid devices more defensible [5, 16, 17, 143].
581: Long-term environmental stability requires particular attention because hydration and chemical degradation are interdependent. Water facilitates ion transport and conformal interfaces but also contributes to evaporation-related failure and can influence MXene oxidation. Oxidation, in turn, alters conductive pathways and active surfaces, while polymer aging or interfacial separation can produce similar electrochemical losses. Polymer wrapping, antioxidant additives, surface-chemistry control, and oxygen-barrier packaging are promising approaches, but excessive isolation of MXene sheets may diminish ion accessibility. Organohydrogels, ionogels, and hygroscopic formulations can improve resistance to drying or freezing, although solvent selection must be assessed against electrolyte conductivity, operating voltage, cost, and biological compatibility. Comparative aging studies should track water content, structural and chemical changes, impedance, and performance under defined temperature, humidity, and oxygen exposure rather than infer stability from cycling retention alone [30, 31, 41, 45, 115].
582: Interfacial and hierarchical architecture design offers a complementary route to resolving the conductivity–compliance trade-off. MXene/graphene oxide or reduced-graphene-oxide frameworks, porous three-dimensional scaffolds, and controlled nanosheet spacing can mitigate restacking and preserve ion-accessible surfaces, but their effects on packing density and mechanical integrity require concurrent evaluation. Within polymer-rich composites, filler concentration, orientation, surface terminations, crosslink density, and reversible hydrogen-bonding or coordination interactions jointly determine electrical percolation, swelling, adhesion, and fatigue resistance. Gradient structures, mechanically compliant interconnects, and electrode placement near the neutral bending plane may reduce strain localization at assembled interfaces. Claims of self-healing should be supported by recovery of electrochemical function and contact resistance after repeated damage and repair, not solely by restored macroscopic shape or tensile properties [25, 42-44, 108, 134].
583: Mechanistic studies will also be needed to identify the rate-controlling processes responsible for failure. Coupling electrochemical impedance spectroscopy and charge–discharge analysis with microscopy, structural characterization, rheology, and measurements under imposed deformation could help separate resistance increases arising from nanosheet oxidation, water redistribution, pore collapse, interfacial delamination, or fractured conductors. Where feasible, in situ or operando measurements should connect these evolving structural features to electrochemical output under realistic cycling and environmental conditions. Such datasets would provide a sounder basis for predictive modeling and artificial-intelligence-assisted optimization of polymer chemistry, MXene surface characteristics, filler loading, and processing. The value of data-driven approaches will depend on consistent metadata, comparable test protocols, representation of failed as well as successful formulations, and validation across independently prepared batches rather than predictions based only on small, heterogeneous datasets [12, 25, 32, 45].
584: Manufacturing research must address the reproducibility and environmental burden of the complete fabrication route. Hazardous fluoride-containing etching chemistries, variations in nanosheet dimensions and surface terminations, dispersion oxidation, and unstable ink rheology complicate translation from laboratory batches to patterned electrodes and larger-area devices. Alternative or safer etching strategies should be assessed alongside yield, conductivity, residual reagent removal, and batch consistency, rather than by synthesis conditions alone. Water-based ink formulation, screen and inkjet printing, extrusion printing, laser patterning, and potentially roll-to-roll-compatible processing offer relevant routes, provided that adhesion, feature uniformity, conductivity after drying, encapsulation quality, and shelf life remain controlled. Quality assurance should extend from precursor and MXene characterization to the electrochemical performance of completed devices [20, 39, 40, 58, 196, 197, 209].
585: At the system level, design targets should be derived from real electrical loads and patterns of mechanical use. Microsupercapacitors are well suited to rapid charge delivery and buffering intermittent harvested energy, whereas hydrogel-based batteries can support longer operation; hybrid systems may combine these functions with photovoltaic, triboelectric, or other harvesting modules. Nevertheless, successful integration demands more than short-duration activation of a sensor or light-emitting diode. Demonstrations should quantify energy input, conversion and rectification losses, charging time, leakage and self-discharge, voltage regulation, series-cell balancing, and the power consumed by sensing, communication, and actuation over representative duty cycles. For soft robots, assessment should also consider the influence of storage placement and packaging stiffness on actuation; for wearable devices, it should include repeated attachment, perspiration, body motion, and practical operating duration [17-20, 160]. 
586: Biomedical and transient applications warrant a distinct evidence threshold. Skin-conformal demonstrations do not establish implantability, and hydrogel biocompatibility does not establish the safety of MXene-containing electrodes, residual synthesis reagents, current collectors, interconnects, or degradation products. Future work should examine material exposure state, nanosheet release, oxidation products, electrical leakage, tissue irritation, and relevant biological responses under intended durations and conditions of contact. For transient devices, the duration of stable power output must be matched to the required monitoring or therapeutic interval, while encapsulation must prevent premature failure yet permit the intended end-of-life process. Testing in physiologically relevant fluids, with appropriate mechanical and sterilization challenges, should quantify residual components and dissolved or fragmented products. Mass loss or visible disappearance alone is insufficient to demonstrate safe, complete-device biodegradation [13-15, 111, 137, 142, 193].
587: Sustainability should similarly be evaluated at the device rather than constituent-material level. Processing chemicals, solvent use, fabrication yield, replacement frequency, repairability, and end-of-life management may alter the value of a material initially described as environmentally favorable. The preferred strategy will depend on the application: long-lived soft robotic modules may benefit from repairable or replaceable components, whereas disposable wearables require practical disposal pathways and temporary biomedical systems require predictable degradation and biological clearance. Life-cycle considerations should be incorporated at the design stage without assuming that a degradable polymer network renders the entire device benign [14, 58, 137, 142, 196, 197].
588: In conclusion, hydrogel–MXene composites offer a versatile materials platform because electronically conductive and electrochemically active nanosheets can be coupled with hydrated, deformable, and multifunctional polymer networks. The reviewed work demonstrates promising routes toward flexible supercapacitors, microsupercapacitors, batteries, and self-powered sensing interfaces, while revealing that conductivity, capacitance, and deformation tolerance do not automatically translate into dependable device operation. Progress will be determined by the ability to preserve coupled ionic–electronic transport and interface integrity through realistic use, establish reproducible full-device metrics, verify biological and environmental outcomes, and manufacture consistent architectures at relevant scales. This materials-to-systems perspective provides a basis for assessing when hydrogel–MXene energy storage can meet the distinct requirements of soft robotics, wearable electronics, smart textiles, and biomedical technologies.
